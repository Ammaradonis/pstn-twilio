Most of this transcript reduces to five ideas you can use: audit your own effort, fix incentives, sell into every conversation you're already having, get cash in first, and test hard. Two parts, the employee paranoia and "legal garbage," would actively hurt a business like yours, so I've reshaped those rather than passing them along as written.

### 1. Find the gap between what you do and what you could do

**From the text:** *"Make a list of all the ways in which you can improve the way you run your business then ask yourself why you don't do it?"*

This is the most useful exercise in the whole piece. The trick is to make the list specific enough that you can't dodge it. "Work harder" is useless; "call 40 schools a day" isn't. Your version might include:

- **More outbound at the right times.** Martial arts owners teach evenings and weekends, so late morning to mid-afternoon is your window. If you're making 10 calls in that window instead of 40, that's the gap.
- **Using the prospect's own missed calls as your pitch.** Call target schools during class hours and log how many go to voicemail. "I called you Tuesday at 6:15 and nobody picked up. That was a parent trying to sign their kid up" beats any generic demo.
- **Following up on every no-show demo within 24 hours.** Most small agencies let these die quietly.
- **Personalized demos.** Record a short clip of your agent answering as *their* school, using their name, programs, and trial class offer.
- **Asking every happy client for two referrals.** Owners know each other through federations, tournaments, and seminars.

Then comes the question he asks: *why aren't you doing it?* The honest answers are usually that you have no daily target, rejection feels bad, or nobody is keeping score. The fix is a simple scoreboard you fill in every day: calls, conversations, demos booked, demos shown, closes.

I'd skip his "18 hours" framing. Sales calls made while exhausted convert worse. What matters is output per focused hour.

### 2. Fix the incentives, including your own

**From the text:** *"The main reason is obviously because you're [not] incentivized to do so"* and the employee who says *"let me make the money first, and if it rolls in, reward me."*

People, including you, do what they're rewarded for. You can apply that in three places.

**For yourself:**
- Tie personal rewards to business numbers: "When I hit 20 active schools, I take the trip."
- Commit publicly to a monthly target with a peer or mastermind so missing it costs you something.

**For anyone working with you:**
- Pay appointment setters per demo that *shows*, not per demo booked, so they stop booking junk.
- Pay closers a bonus when a school is still active at month three, which rewards selling to schools that will stay.
- Adopt his "prove it first" deal as a standing offer: "Bring me a revenue idea. If it works, you get X% of what it brings in for six months." A setter might say BJJ gyms convert better than karate schools and want to focus there, or a support person might suggest an SMS follow-up add-on. Let them run it and share the upside.

**For your customers.** This is the same logic pointed outward, and it's powerful in your niche:
- Offer a performance guarantee: "If the agent doesn't book at least X trial classes in 60 days, your next month is free."
- Or charge partly per trial class booked, so the owner only pays more when they're making more.

That's the employee's pitch turned into a sales offer: *let me make you money first.*

### 3. Sell into conversations you're already having

**From the Vixels story:** while selling websites at N2, he spotted companies big enough for TV ads and passed them to his brother. Leave aside the part where he did it on his employer's time. The mechanism is sound: every conversation is a chance to spot a second sale.

**Adjacent offers you could add or partner on:**
- A school with a missed-call problem almost always has a lead follow-up problem too, so sell an SMS/email nurture tier that chases unbooked inquiries.
- If a school's website is bad, a web designer partner can pay you a referral fee.
- If a school has multiple locations or belongs to an association, that's an enterprise or group deal instead of one account.

**Using other people's conversations:**
- Martial arts management software companies (Spark Membership, Kicksite, Gymdesk, and similar) talk to your exact customers daily, which makes them natural referral or integration partners.
- Seminar instructors and tournament organizers are in front of dozens of owners at once.
- Your own clients talk to other owners, so a referral credit turns every client into a salesperson.

**Adjacent verticals later:** dance studios, swim schools, and kids' gymnastics run the same model of trial classes, parents calling, and owners busy teaching. Only expand once martial arts is working, though. Focus is part of why a niche offer sells.

### 4. Get cash flowing before building more

**From the text:** *"Is your money coming in? Yes or no?"* and *"get the money in even if none of the fulfillment is ready."*

The useful part is not waiting until everything is perfect before asking for money. Pre-selling is how you fund growth. Some ways to do it:
- Sell "founding school" spots at a locked-in rate, paid upfront, before building a new integration.
- Before building a feature like booking directly into a school's CRM, get three schools to put down a deposit. If nobody pays, you just saved weeks.
- Charge a setup fee at signing instead of waiting on monthly billing.
- Offer an annual prepay discount, which puts cash in the bank now.

Where I'd push back: upfront money only works when you're honest about delivery dates and refunds. Taking money for something you can't deliver leads to chargebacks, and the martial arts community is small enough that one angry owner in a Facebook group can cost you ten sales.

"Legal garbage" is also the wrong frame for AI phone agents specifically. Your product records and processes calls, often from parents asking about kids' classes. Call-recording consent rules, data protection (GDPR if you serve schools in Europe), and rules about telling callers they're talking to an AI all apply directly to you. Getting this right is cheap. Getting it wrong can end the business. I'm not a lawyer, so it's worth an hour with one where you operate. You can also turn compliance into a selling point: "Fully compliant, so you're protected."

### 5. Handle employee disengagement with visibility, not paranoia

**From the text:** employees with side hustles will *"disappear,"* so *"these motherfuckers need to get laid off."*

Look at his own story. He read Wikipedia three days a week and nobody noticed. The failure there wasn't that side hustles exist; his employer simply wasn't measuring output. So the useful lesson is to make output visible and give good people a reason to stay:
- Give every role a clear weekly number: setters track demos shown, onboarding tracks days to go-live, support tracks issues resolved.
- Hold a short weekly check-in on those numbers. Disengagement shows up in the data long before someone quits.
- If you have an entrepreneurial rep, give them ownership of something, like a new vertical or region, with profit share. You keep the hustle working for you instead of against you.
- Use sensible contracts with confidentiality and non-solicitation clauses. How enforceable they are depends on the country, and in many places you can't fire someone simply for having a side business.
- If you use commission-only setters, assume they sell other things too. Your job is to make your offer the one most worth their time.

### 6. Run aggressive tests

**From the text:** *"Grind and seek pain with crazy tests."* Read that as uncomfortable experiments, not mistreating customers. Run one test per week and measure it:
- **Niche:** compare BJJ gyms, kids' karate schools, and MMA gyms to see which closes fastest and churns least.
- **Offer:** compare a paid 30-day pilot, a free 14-day trial, and a performance guarantee.
- **Channel:** compare cold calling, walking into schools in person, cold email with a recorded demo, and owner Facebook groups.
- **Price:** try a higher setup fee with a lower monthly, and the reverse.
- **Script:** open with the missed-call stat versus opening with revenue lost per missed trial signup.

---

### How to make this stick

Put it into a weekly rhythm rather than rereading the transcript:
- **Monday:** update your gap list and pick this week's one test.
- **Daily:** fill in the scoreboard.
- **Friday:** review the numbers and the test result, and decide whether to keep it, kill it, or scale it. Check that cash collected is going up.
