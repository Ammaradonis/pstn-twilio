Here's how to turn this into how your business actually runs. One part of the text contradicts itself when you apply it to your business, and I'll cover that too, because it matters more than anything else here.

---

## Point 1: Outsource cheaply, and use students and Fiverr first

**The text says:** *"Nearly anything you need done, a student can do... students are broke which means they work cheap."*

**How to internalize it:** Before you pay for anything, ask one question: *"Is there a student or solo freelancer who could do 80% of this for 20% of the price?"* Make that question automatic.

**Examples in an AI phone agent business:**

- **Demo and marketing videos → film student.** You need a 60-second video showing a parent calling a dojo at 7pm during kids' class, the owner too busy to answer, and your AI agent booking a trial class. A production company might charge thousands. A film student will do it for a few hundred and a portfolio piece. They can also film testimonials at client schools.
- **Integrations → computer science student.** Your agent is more valuable if it connects to the schools' membership and booking software (Zen Planner, Spark Membership, Kicksite, Gymdesk, and so on). A final-year CS student can build and maintain those connectors, possibly as a capstone project.
- **Call scripts and conversation flows → communications or linguistics student.** Someone has to write and test how the agent handles "how much are classes?", "is it safe for my 6-year-old?", and "do you do BJJ or just karate?" Students are good at running 50 test calls and noting where the agent sounds robotic.
- **Lead lists → marketing or business student.** Building a spreadsheet of 500 martial arts schools with owner names, websites, and whether they currently miss calls is tedious, cheap work that's perfect for a student.
- **Fiverr jobs.** Use it for your logo, landing page tweaks, ad creatives, translating agent scripts if you serve schools in other languages, and professional voice samples.
- **The niece or nephew version.** Your client schools are full of teenagers and college-age black belts. A 19-year-old assistant instructor studying business already knows the culture, the vocabulary, and the owners' pain points. That person could become your best part-time salesperson or onboarding assistant.

## Point 2: Build a structure of individuals under you, not companies

**The text says:** *"You want this person who's your assistant, this person who's your salesman, this person who does your app development... and it's ALL PEOPLE."* And: *"if an individual has a company, fine that's still an individual."*

**How to internalize it:** Draw your org chart. Every box should have a person's name in it, not a company's logo. Where a box does say "company," you should be able to say why only a company can do it.

**What your chart could look like:**

- **You (CEO).** You handle vision, key client relationships, and pricing.
- **Salesperson.** Ideally someone who trains or has taught martial arts, paid mostly on commission. School owners trust someone who knows what a belt test or a trial-class funnel is.
- **Agent builder.** A student or freelancer who configures the voice agents, writes prompts, and fixes issues.
- **Onboarding assistant.** A part-time student who loads each new school's class schedule, pricing, instructors, and FAQs into the agent.
- **Cybersecurity student.** Reviews your setup against a checklist once a quarter. See the warning below.
- **Lawyer.** This one should be a real licensed solo lawyer, not a student. A one-person practice counts as "an individual with a company," so it fits the text.
- **Advisor.** The text mentions a former official. In your world, that could be a retired federation official, a well-known instructor, or someone who owns five successful schools. They open doors and tell you what owners actually care about.

**Where the text itself allows a company** (*"if you need something very specific and personalized and you need a really big company, okay do it"*):

- **Telephony and voice AI infrastructure** (Twilio, Vapi, Retell, or whatever you run on). Don't have a student build phone infrastructure from scratch.
- **Payments.** Use Stripe or similar, not something custom.
- **Insurance.** Get liability or errors-and-omissions coverage in case an agent gives a parent wrong information.

## Point 3: Individuals have one job, companies have two

**The text says:** *"The person who's tasked with the website has one job which is to make my website. The company... has two jobs: 1) make the website 2) find more people who need websites."*

**How to internalize it:** The text says individuals have a "vested interest," but it never explains how you create one. Cheap people don't automatically care. You make them care through how you pay and treat them.

**Examples of building vested interest:**

- **Salesperson:** a commission on each signed school, plus a smaller monthly bonus for every month that school stays. Now they care about selling schools that will stick, not just any school.
- **Agent builder:** a bonus tied to client retention or call-handling quality, such as the percentage of calls that end in a booked trial.
- **Onboarding student:** a small bonus when a school goes live with zero setup complaints in its first two weeks.
- **Advisor:** a small profit share or a referral fee for each school they introduce.
- **Everyone:** public credit. Their name goes on the case study, their work goes in their portfolio, and you write the LinkedIn recommendation. For students this is sometimes worth as much as the money.

This is what the text means by *"If you're a good leader, your staff genuinely want everything to work."* The pay structure is what makes the leadership real.

## Where the text is incomplete: risks of the student model

The text is right that this is cheaper. It skips the costs, and in your business some of them are serious.

**Students leave.** They graduate, have exams, or move away.
- Record every setup process as a screen-recorded video and written checklist, so the next student can take over in a day.
- Keep a shared prompt library, so a new builder isn't starting from zero.

**You must own everything.**
- All code lives in *your* GitHub, all agents in *your* platform account, all domains and logins under *your* email. If the student who built your integration disappears, you still have it.
- Get a written contractor agreement that assigns intellectual property to you. Without one, in many places the person who wrote the code may legally own it.

**Your business handles sensitive data.** Your agents record calls from parents, often including children's names and ages.
- Don't give a student unsupervised access to real call recordings or customer data.
- Depending on where your schools are, there may be call-recording consent laws, data protection rules (GDPR if you serve EU schools), and AI disclosure rules requiring callers to be told they're talking to an AI. The EU AI Act has provisions on this. This is exactly why the lawyer in your chart needs to be a licensed professional.
- A cybersecurity student is a good extra set of eyes, but not your only line of defense.

**"Cheap" isn't the same as "cheapest."** A student paid fairly stays for two years. A student paid as little as possible leaves after two months, taking your knowledge with them. That churn is expensive.

**Classification.** If a "contractor" works fixed hours under your direction, the law in many countries may treat them as an employee. Ask your lawyer once, early.

## The most important part: you are the company in this story

From a martial arts school owner's point of view, *they* are the hustler at the top, and *your business* is the company below them sending an invoice. Everything the text says about bad companies is what your prospects are afraid of when they hear your pitch.

The last paragraph you included takes the company's side:

> *"If I have to choose between delivering my services on time or taking on more services and taking more money, I'll take the money... let them wait until I collect some new MONEY."*

The rest of the text describes that exact attitude as the reason companies are bad to work with. It's also a losing strategy for your specific business, for three reasons:

1. **You sell a subscription.** Your money comes from schools paying month after month. If you make a school wait and it cancels after three months, you've lost more than you gained by rushing to sign the next one. In recurring revenue, keeping clients *is* cash flow.
2. **Your failures cost your clients money immediately.** If the agent gives the wrong class time or fails to book a trial, that's a lost student for the owner, and they'll notice.
3. **Martial arts owners talk to each other** at seminars, tournaments, in business coaching groups, and in owner Facebook groups. One angry owner can cost you ten prospects.

### Turn the text into your competitive advantage instead

The text gives you a perfect sales angle: be the individual, not the company.

- **Pitch:** "You're not ticket number 400 at some agency. You get my direct number, and I personally make sure your agent works."
- **Onboarding:** Do the first setup call for your first 20 to 30 schools yourself. It builds trust and teaches you what owners actually need.
- **Pricing tied to their success:** Offer a guarantee or pricing linked to booked trial classes. That shows you care whether their school grows, which is the opposite of *"If you've paid the invoice, they don't give a shit."*
- **Monthly check-in:** Send each school a short report ("Your agent answered 143 calls and booked 22 trials this month"), so they feel your attention.

### Getting cash flow without abandoning clients

The cash-flow instinct is valid. Here's how to act on it without becoming the company the text criticizes:

- **Charge a setup fee upfront.** Cash comes in on signing, before you've done the work.
- **Offer an annual prepay discount.** A school that pays for 12 months upfront gives you a big cash injection, and you've earned it by delivering.
- **Use a waitlist instead of overloading.** If your onboarding student can set up three schools a week properly, sign three and put the rest on a waitlist. Scarcity helps sales, and quality stays high.
- **Ask for referrals.** Happy schools bring in new schools at almost no cost to you, which is cash flow *through* your clients, not at their expense.

## A daily test to internalize all of this

Whenever you're about to spend money or hand off work, run through four questions:

1. **Can an individual do this instead of a company?** (Point 2)
2. **Can a student or Fiverr freelancer do it cheaper?** (Point 1)
3. **What's their vested interest, and how am I paying for it?** (Point 3)
4. **If they vanished tomorrow, do I still own the work, the logins, and the data?** (The missing risks)

And whenever you make a decision about a client, ask a fifth: **"Would I, as a school owner, feel like this company actually cares whether my school succeeds?"** If the answer is no, you're becoming the company the text warns about, and your competitors who stay "individual" will take your clients.

For your AI phone-agent company, I would condense the entire passage into this operating philosophy:

### Use individuals before agencies when quality is comparable.

### Keep fixed costs low.

### Build a trusted network of specialists.

### Outsource support functions.

### Retain control of customer relationships.

### Protect cash aggressively.

### Never outsource your core advantage.

If you apply those principles consistently, you'll capture most of the useful insight from the passage while avoiding the biggest mistake in it: assuming that every company is misaligned and every cheap contractor is a better choice. In practice, the best approach is to match the provider—individual, freelancer, boutique firm, or larger company—to the risk and importance of the task.
