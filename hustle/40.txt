## First, the line itself

He says he doesn't know if it's true. It isn't. The crisis-equals-opportunity thing comes from the Chinese 危機 (*wēijī*), and the second character means something closer to "crucial point" than "opportunity." Japanese uses the same compound (*kiki*) and it just means crisis.

Say it anyway if it fires you up, but not in a sales call. You sell to karate, judo, and aikido schools. A decent share of those owners have spent twenty years around Japanese terminology and some of them speak it. Getting corrected on that by a prospect costs you more than the line is worth.

The idea underneath it is sound regardless of the etymology.

---

## What he's actually doing (the part to internalize)

Strip the swearing out and there are four moves, in order:

**1. Speed, then twist.** He says it in passing and it's the most important sequencing in the whole thing. Stop the bleeding first, monetize second. These are separate motions. If you try to monetize before you've fixed it, you're the vulture, not the fixer. The twist only pays because you were the one who was reliable during the mess.

**2. Find who just lost something.** Every chaos event creates a group of people with a new, urgent, specific need they did not have last week. That need has a price attached and no established supplier.

**3. Find who has a surplus of exactly that thing**, and stand between them. The merch story is the purest version. He didn't make hats. He didn't buy inventory. He knew ten YouTubers who suddenly needed merch and one company that wanted volume, and he charged for being the bridge. Zero outlay.

**4. Everything feeds back to Money In.** A twist that produces "brand awareness" is not a twist. It has to produce a payment with a date on it.

The backend story is the same four moves applied internally. His backend was empty (the chaos), customers had a surplus of willingness-to-buy-if-cheap-enough (the resource), the 99c offer was the bridge, and he broke even on the transaction while gaining the asset he actually needed.

**That's the key insight people miss about the face cream story: he wasn't selling face cream. He was buying backend data with a discount.** He paid for an asset using margin instead of cash.

## The five questions (make this reflexive)

When anything goes wrong, in your business or your market, run these in under five minutes:

1. Who just lost something? (money, students, staff, software, certainty, a supplier)
2. What do they now urgently need that they didn't need last week?
3. Who has a surplus of exactly that?
4. Can I stand between them and charge for the bridge?
5. What version of this can I launch in 48 hours with zero outlay?

Question 5 is the discipline. Most "opportunities" die because the play required building something. His plays required a phone call.

## Drill 1: The Chaos Log

One sheet, four columns: **What broke / Who got hurt / What I sell because of it / Live by when.**

Rules: entry goes in within 24 hours of the chaos, offer goes live within 7 days, and you're not allowed to complain about something out loud until column three is filled in. That last rule is the whole training program. It converts the emotional reflex (frustration) into a mechanical one (scanning).

## Drill 2: The Chaos Radar

He improvises brilliantly but assumes the news reaches him. In your market it won't unless you go get it. Thirty minutes a week:

- School owner Facebook groups and the gym-software user groups (this is where price hikes and outages get screamed about first)
- Status pages and changelogs for Zen Planner, PushPress, Kicksite, Gymdesk, Mindbody
- Google Maps in your target metros, filtered for "permanently closed" gyms
- Your own churn calls and lost-deal calls, which are chaos reports you're already being handed
- Local news and tournament circuit chatter

You cannot twist chaos you never heard about. Deal flow follows information flow.

## Drill 3: Pre-write the plays for chaos you know is coming

This is the honest upgrade on the source material. He's improvising off the top of his head and it's impressive, but improvising under pressure is the amateur version. Some chaos is on a calendar: January signup floods, the summer bleed, contract renewal season, the annual CRM price increase. Write those plays now, in a quiet week, so that when it hits you're executing instead of inventing.

## Chaos inside your business (the "backend" plays)

**Your empty backend is proof, not data.** You almost certainly lack a library of real case studies, real recorded calls, and hard numbers from martial arts schools specifically. That is your missing backend. So run his 99c move:

- Pick one metro. Offer 20 schools a 30-day pilot at $1, or free, on the condition that you get call recordings, before/after booked-trial numbers, and a video testimonial. You break even or slightly lose. What you gain: 20 case studies, a transcript library of thousands of real martial arts phone calls (which makes your agent measurably better at your niche than any generic AI receptionist), and 20 accounts already in the habit of being billed by you. Same structure as "99c if you buy with any other product." The discount has a condition attached that buys you the asset.
- Variant: charge $99 for the pilot instead of $1, specifically because a paid pilot converts far better than a free one and the friction filters out tire-kickers. You're still buying the backend, just at a better price.

**Your agent botches a client's calls for a day.** Speed: fix it, call the owner personally before they call you, credit them. Twist options:
- Turn the incident into a **Call Quality Audit** you now run monthly and charge for. You built the QA tooling to fix the bug; now it's a product tier.
- Trade the credit for a case study: "We screwed up, we fixed it in four hours, let me film you saying that." A testimonial about how you handled a failure outsells a testimonial about how great you are.
- Publish the postmortem publicly. Every competitor in AI voice hides their failures. Being the one who posts the incident report is a sales asset in a market where owners are scared of handing the phone to a robot.

**A big client churns.** Speed: exit interview, same day. Twist:
- Don't lose them, down-sell them. Build the cheap self-serve tier you were going to build anyway and put the churned account on it. Reduced revenue beats zero revenue and the tier now exists as a product.
- You now have open capacity. Run a "founding member" cohort to fill it, same mechanic as the backend fill.
- Three churns with the same reason is a product roadmap handed to you for free. Ship the fix, then call every past churn: "You left because of X. X is gone. Come back at your old price." Win-back campaigns close at rates cold outbound never will.

**Your model provider raises prices.** Twist: this is the single best excuse for a cash injection you will ever get. Email every client: "Prices go up on the 1st. Prepay 12 months at the old rate before then." You get a year of cash in the door now, and clients get a win. Same move works on any supplier increase.

## Chaos in the martial arts market (where the real money is)

He's right that market chaos beats internal chaos. Internal chaos gets you back to zero. Market chaos hands you a pile of desperate buyers with an urgent problem and no supplier.

**A head instructor defects and takes 40 students to open a rival gym.** This happens constantly in martial arts and it is devastating to the school that loses them. New, urgent need: get those students back, fast, before their new habit sets. You have an outbound calling system.
- Sell a **Defection Recovery Campaign**, priced per reactivated student rather than monthly. $150 per student who returns and stays 30 days. On 40 students you're pitching into a five-figure outcome for the owner, and your cost is compute.
- Once you've run it twice, it becomes a standing product: the lapsed-member reactivation campaign, sold to every school, no defection required. Every school has 200 dead names in their CRM.

**A local school closes suddenly.** Fifty to three hundred students are orphaned mid-contract, most of them wanting to keep training.
- Call every school within a 15-minute drive and sell an absorption campaign: your agent handles the inbound spike and does outbound to the orphaned roster where the closing owner will share or sell the list. Price per enrollment.
- Do this with some grace. Martial arts in any given metro is a small, reputational world where all the owners see each other at tournaments. Helping students land somewhere is a good look. Being visibly gleeful about a closure will follow you around for years.

**January.** Not a disaster, but it's the same shape: a flood the school cannot handle. Owners miss 40 to 60 percent of inbound calls in January because they're on the mat teaching the people who already signed up.
- Sell the entire year off this one month. "You will miss roughly X calls in January. At your average member value that's Y dollars on the floor." Sell a January-only package in November, then convert to annual in February on the back of the numbers you just produced.
- The mirror play: summer is the bleed. Sell an outbound retention and summer-camp-filler campaign to the same schools in April.

**Their lead-gen agency stops working.** This is your exact Adpocalypse. Martial arts schools get sold $2–3k/month Meta ad packages. When ad costs spike, accounts get banned, or targeting degrades, those schools are suddenly paying for leads that produce nothing.
- Flip your pitch from "more leads" to "you're already paying for leads and 50% of them never get a human on the phone." That's a cheaper, higher-ROI message that lands precisely when their ads are failing.
- **The broker version, which is the merch play exactly:** call the martial arts lead-gen agencies. Their clients are complaining that leads don't convert. That's not an ad problem, it's a speed-to-lead problem, and you fix it. Offer to be their white-labeled answering layer. They resell you, or they refer and you pay them a cut. One phone call, no outlay, and you're distributing through someone else's client base. You can run this with five agencies at once.

**A gym management CRM has a price hike, a bad migration, an acquisition, or a sunset.** Every time this happens a few thousand school owners are simultaneously furious and shopping.
- Build the integration, publish a migration guide, run a switch campaign into the angry crowd. You're not selling AI to them at that moment, you're selling relief, and the AI comes attached.
- Longer game: be the one with integrations into all of them, so that whichever one implodes next, you're the stable thing in the stack.

**A staffing crunch or a downturn.** Front desk is the first role a school cuts. That's your best pitch that exists.
- "Your front desk cost $2,400 a month. I'm a fraction of that and I don't call in sick or miss the 8pm callback." Recessions are when your category stops being a novelty and starts being a cost line.

**Chaos in AI itself.** Voice scam headlines, deepfake panic, new state laws requiring AI disclosure on calls, TCPA enforcement. This is chaos in *your* market and most of your competitors will hide from it.
- Be compliant first and loudly. "Disclosure-compliant AI receptionist" with a one-page compliance summary the owner can hand to their lawyer. Fear in a market is a pricing opportunity for whoever resolves it.
- When a sloppy competitor gets bad press, that's the week you run your compliance campaign.

## The merch play, applied to you (your biggest untapped one)

Re-read what he actually did: he had relationships with ten YouTubers and monetized the relationships, not the product. He became the toll booth.

You are accumulating relationships with martial arts school owners. Every one of them buys things: mats, gis and uniforms, payment processing, insurance, tournament software, CRM, marketing services, merch. Right now you make money from exactly one of those.

- Pick three suppliers your schools already need. Call each: "I work with N schools. I can route them to you. I want 5–10% of what they spend." Zero outlay, one call, exactly his play.
- Build the thing that makes the calls easy: a small private group or newsletter for martial arts school owners. It gives you distribution, it gives you the chaos radar from Drill 2, and it makes you the person suppliers have to come through.
- The version with the most leverage: your agent is already on the phone with the school's members. Referral arrangements with adjacent services those members buy become an additional revenue line off infrastructure you already run.

One condition: disclose the cut. These owners talk to each other at every tournament. A broker arrangement everyone knows about is a business. A hidden one, discovered, ends you in that market permanently.

## The Money In filter

Every entry in your Chaos Log has to resolve to a sentence in this form: **"This produces [amount] from [specific buyer] by [date]."** If you can't write that sentence, it's not a twist, it's a distraction. He's ruthless about this and it's why his examples all end with "bang" instead of a strategy document.

## Where this gets people killed

Four failure modes, worth knowing before you run the playbook:

- **Trust is the currency the twist spends.** Calling a competitor's stranded clients is fair game. Dancing on a local gym's grave in a community where every owner knows every other owner is not, and it's unrecoverable.
- **Break-even offers train discounts.** His 99c offer had conditions on it for a reason (bundled with another product). Always time-box it and reason-box it, so the price drop is attached to an event, not to you.
- **The twist has to reuse an existing asset.** His merch play worked because he was already a YouTuber with relationships. Chaos plays that require you to build a new business are just distractions wearing a hustle costume. Yours should reuse the phone system or the school relationships. If it does neither, skip it.
- **Outbound calling has real legal texture.** The reactivation and absorption campaigns are the highest-value plays here and also the ones with exposure: TCPA, consent, state laws that require disclosing an AI caller. Get written authorization from the school, call only their existing relationships, and check disclosure rules per state. Do this properly and it becomes a selling point, since your sloppier competitors can't say the same.

First, one correction: the famous claim that "chaos" and "opportunity" are the same word in Japanese is not actually true. It's a popular business myth derived from a misunderstanding of the Chinese word 危机 (crisis), which combines concepts related to danger and a critical point—not literally "danger + opportunity."

# The Mental Model

Most people think:

**Chaos → Fix problem**

The mindset from the text is:

**Chaos → Fix problem + Monetize consequence**

Whenever something goes wrong, ask:

1. What broke?
2. Who is now losing money?
3. Who is now wasting time?
4. Who is now frustrated?
5. Who is now scared?
6. Who suddenly needs a solution?

The answer usually contains a sales opportunity.
