## What the trick actually is

Strip away the pickup stuff and the mechanic is this: **you change the question the prospect is answering.**

Left alone, a school owner on your call is answering *"Should I buy this?"* That question has a built-in defense — "no" is free and safe.

When you talk about the future in specific detail, you force their brain to answer a different question: *"What will it be like when I have this?"* To picture it at all, they have to simulate owning it. And once they've simulated owning it, "no" starts to feel like taking something away instead of not getting something. The decision quietly moves from *whether* to *what it'll be like*.

The second mechanic is commitment. The moment they *answer* a future-framed question — even to correct you — they've accepted the premise. In the text, the girl laughing "when we're married hahaha" is the tell. She engaged with the frame. Same thing when a school owner says "ha, well if we open the second location..." He just told you he's thinking about it as his.

## One change I'd make before you run this

The person in that transcript is selling a $17 ebook and one-off TV ad buys. You're selling recurring software to a community where every owner knows twenty other owners, goes to the same seminars, and sits in the same Facebook groups.

That annotation about lying and promising fancy shit will kill you specifically. If you tell a guy "in a few months we'll be doing automated belt-test reminders" and you never build it, he churns at month three and tells the whole association. Your CAC is dead.

The good news: you don't need to lie, because you have a *real* future to sell that's better than an invented one. You have a product that improves, a relationship with a recurring cadence, and a customer whose business grows in predictable seasons. Future-pace things you will actually do. The frame does all the same work and you get to keep the client.

## The mechanics

### 1. "When," never "if"

This is 80% of it and it costs nothing. Go through your entire pitch and hunt every "if."

- Weak: "If you go with us, the agent would answer during class times."
- Strong: "When we go live, the agent's picking up during your 4pm kids class, so Sensei Mike isn't jogging off the mat."

- Weak: "If this works out, we could look at the second location."
- Strong: "When you open Northside, we just clone the agent and swap the schedule. Takes an afternoon."

- Weak: "If you want, we can pull call transcripts."
- Strong: "When you're looking at January's numbers, you'll have every call transcript sitting in there. You'll finally see what parents actually ask before they book."

### 2. Give them a job in the future

This is the strongest one in the transcript ("screenshot it and send it to me"). It works because it makes them mentally rehearse a specific moment *after* the purchase, and it flatters them by making them a collaborator.

- "When you get your first trial booked off a 9pm Sunday call, screenshot the confirmation and send it to me. I'm collecting those for the owners' group and yours will be a good one because of your Little Dragons volume."
- "When the agent hits a question it can't answer — and it will, every school has two or three weird ones — text me the transcript and I'll add it that day."
- "After your first month, tell me which of your programs is generating the most calls. I'm building a benchmark across schools and nobody's got clean data on the kids-vs-adults split."
- "When a parent asks about the summer camp waitlist, forward me how the agent handled it. Camp calls are the ones I want to tune hardest."

Notice what every one of those does: it assumes he has the product, it assumes he's paying attention to it, and it assumes he's still talking to you in 30 days.

### 3. Anchor to a date that's already in his calendar

Vague futures are weak. Dated futures are strong, and martial arts schools run on a brutally predictable calendar. Keep ten of these loaded:

- January enrollment surge
- Back-to-school, late August
- Summer camp signups, March–April
- The next belt test / graduation
- Their association's annual event or the SuperShow
- The week they add a second Little Dragons class

Use them:

- "We'll be live well before January. That's really the month this thing pays for itself — most schools we work with are missing 40% of January calls because everyone's on the mat."
- "Get this running now and by the time summer camp signups hit in March, you've got three months of transcripts telling you exactly what parents are asking about camp."
- "When you're at the SuperShow in July, you'll be the guy telling the other owners what your missed-call number went to."

### 4. Sell the big deal while you're closing the small one

This is the 19k/500k move. Your version: you're closing one school at a few hundred a month, but you're talking about the multi-location rollout, the franchise deal, the association-wide thing.

- "Down the line, when you've got three locations, we'll set it up so the agent routes by zip code and the parent never knows which school picked up. That's the version I'm most excited about."
- "The schools that have been with me longest, we're now doing reactivation campaigns with — the agent calls the 200 people who quit in the last two years. That's probably a next-year conversation for you, but it's where this goes."
- "Eventually if you wanted to put this in front of your association, I'd do a group rate. Let's get yours dialed in first so you've got real numbers to show them."

He's sitting there nodding about the association deal while signing the one-location contract. Same trick as the sponsorships before and after the programs.

### 5. Future-pace the objection instead of arguing with it

Don't rebut. Answer from inside a future where he already owns it.

**"My members want to talk to a real person."**
Weak: "Studies show 70% of callers prefer..."
Strong: "They do. That's why the agent hands off to your cell for anything that isn't scheduling or pricing. What you'll notice around week two is that almost nobody's asking for a human — they're asking 'do you have a 5-year-old class' and 'how much is it,' and they just want the answer at 9pm."

**"I need to think about it."**
Weak: "What's holding you back?"
Strong: "Sure. One thing to think about while you're deciding — count your missed calls this week in your phone log. When we talk Thursday I want to run the math on what those were worth at your average member value. My guess is it's six or seven grand a year, but you'll know better than me."

**"It's expensive."**
Strong: "When you look at it in March after camp signups, you're not going to be comparing it to your phone bill. You're going to be comparing it to what one extra kid on a family plan is worth over three years."

### 6. Close into logistics, not into a yes

The assumptive close is just future-pacing 48 hours out.

- "So next step — I'll need your class schedule and your pricing sheet. Are you on Zen Planner or Kicksite? That changes how we do the booking handoff."
- "Wednesday's usually my build day. If I get your FAQ answers by Tuesday night you'll be live for the weekend, which is when you're getting the most calls anyway."

If he goes along with the logistics question, he's bought. If he stops you, you've surfaced the real objection earlier than you would have.

## How to internalize it

Reading this won't do it. Three drills:

**Rewrite your deck and your email sequence in one sitting.** Every conditional becomes a "when." Do it once and the muscle starts forming.

**Count on your own calls.** Record them, and count how many future-tense sentences you said. Most people are at zero to two. Get to eight or ten per call. That's the only metric that matters at first.

**Build a written bank of twenty future statements** specific to martial arts schools — five seasonal, five growth/expansion, five homework assignments, five roadmap. Memorize them cold. The reason this is hard live is that you're busy handling the conversation and can't invent futures on the fly. If they're pre-loaded, you just drop them in.

---

## Where it backfires

Two places, and they're worth knowing.

**Future-pacing before they've agreed there's a problem.** If the owner doesn't yet believe he's missing calls, talking about January makes you sound like you're pitching past a "no." Get agreement on the pain first — get him to look at his actual call log — then future-pace. Order matters.

**Futures that are too far out for the price point.** At a few hundred a month, "two years from now" is fine as color but your primary anchors should be 30, 60, 90 days. The TV guy could stretch to two years because he was closing $19k. Your load-bearing future is his next enrollment season.

The core idea in that transcript is **not “lie about the future.”** The useful principle is:

> People make decisions based on the future they believe is possible.
>
> When you talk concretely about the future outcome of a working relationship, you help them mentally experience ownership before they've committed.

The persuasive mechanism is called **future pacing**. It's used in sales, consulting, coaching, therapy, recruiting, and negotiations.

The unethical version is:

* Inventing outcomes.
* Promising things you can't deliver.
* Creating false certainty.

The effective version is:

* Painting a believable future.
* Showing the next stages of the relationship.
* Making success feel tangible and specific.

For AI phone agents serving martial arts schools, this is extremely powerful because you're not really selling an AI receptionist.

You're selling:

* More trial appointments.
* More students.
* Less admin work.
* Better retention.
* A larger school.

# Principle 1: Sell the destination, not the tool

Most agency owners say:

> "We provide AI phone agents that answer missed calls."

Future-oriented version:

> "Three months from now, when a parent calls at 8 PM after work, your AI agent will book that intro lesson automatically instead of sending them to voicemail."

Notice what happened.

You didn't discuss the software.

You discussed a future reality.

### Example 1

Instead of:

> "The AI can answer FAQs."

Say:

> "By next month, your staff won't be spending an hour every day answering questions about schedules, uniforms, and pricing because the AI will handle those automatically."

### Example 2

Instead of:

> "The AI integrates with your CRM."

Say:

> "Once we're connected to your CRM, you'll be able to see exactly which campaigns generate the most trial bookings and where your enrollments are coming from."

### Example 3

Instead of:

> "We provide appointment booking."

Say:

> "A few months from now, you'll wake up and see trial lessons booked overnight while the school was closed."

# Principle 2: Presume a successful implementation

The transcript is describing a psychological pattern:

> Talk as though the implementation phase is already underway.

Not:

> "If you buy."

But:

> "When we're setting this up..."

This removes mental friction.

### Example 1

Instead of:

> "If you decide to move forward..."

Say:

> "When we build the call flows, I'll want to see how you currently handle trial lesson bookings."

### Example 2

Instead of:

> "If you become a client..."

Say:

> "During the first two weeks after launch, we'll review the call recordings together and tighten up the scripts."

### Example 3

Instead of:

> "If the AI is installed..."

Say:

> "After the AI has handled a few hundred calls, we'll know exactly where parents are dropping off and where we can improve conversions."

The prospect starts imagining ownership.

# Principle 4: Talk about post-purchase behavior

This was the ebook example in the transcript.

The seller talked about what the buyer would do after consuming the product.

You can do the same thing.

### Example 1

> "When parents start mentioning that they got immediate answers at night, let me know because I collect those stories as case studies."

### Example 2

The prospect unconsciously imagines already being a customer.

# Principle 5: Expand their identity

The strongest future pacing isn't about features.

It's about who they become.

A martial arts school owner doesn't want AI.

They want to become:

* A larger school owner.
* A less stressed owner.
* A more professional operator.
* A business that scales.

### Weak

> "The AI answers calls."

### Strong

> "Eventually you'll stop worrying about missed calls entirely because lead handling will be a system instead of a daily task."

### Weak

> "The AI books appointments."

### Strong

> "As enrollment grows, you'll be able to focus on coaching and running the school rather than chasing phone calls."

### Weak

> "We automate follow-up."

### Strong

> "Six months from now you'll know that every lead gets the same professional follow-up process whether you're on the mats, teaching class, or on vacation."

# Principle 6: Use future-pacing questions

These are extremely effective.

Instead of pitching, get them imagining.

### Examples

> "When trial bookings increase, what's your capacity before you need another instructor?"

> "Once every missed call is answered, where do you think the next bottleneck in your enrollment process will be?"

> "If we added ten extra trial lessons a month, what would that mean for student count by next year?"

> "When the front desk isn't interrupted by phone calls all day, what would you rather have them focusing on?"

These questions force them to mentally live in the future.

---

# Principle 7: Make the future specific

Vague futures are weak.

### Weak

> "You'll grow your business."

### Better

> "Imagine it's three months from now and you can pull up a dashboard showing 127 calls handled, 38 trial lessons booked, and exactly how many became paying students."

### Weak

> "The AI will save time."

### Better

> "Think about next semester's enrollment push. Instead of returning 20 missed calls manually, every lead is contacted immediately and booked automatically."

Specificity creates belief.

# A Martial Arts School Sales Script Example

A complete future-paced section might sound like:

> "When we launch this, the first thing you'll notice is that missed calls stop being a problem. Within the first month we'll see exactly how many trial lessons are being booked through the AI. After a few months we'll have enough call data to optimize the conversations and improve conversion rates. By that point, your staff won't be spending time answering the same questions every day, and you'll have a predictable system for handling leads whether the school is open or closed. As enrollment grows, we can start adding more advanced workflows for retention, reactivation, and student communication."

Notice:

* No hype.
* No fake promises.
* No lying.
* No pressure.

Just a believable future that naturally assumes a continuing relationship.

That's the highest-integrity way to apply the concept from the transcript to your AI phone agent business.
