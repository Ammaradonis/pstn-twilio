## First, decode what actually happened in the Star Wars campaign

Most people read that thread and see "be a jerk, go viral." That's not the mechanic. The mechanic is that he worked **backwards from the product to the enemy**.

The chain was:

1. Product: a course on getting women
2. Buyer: men with disposable income and no women
3. Problem: you can't target "insecure man with money" on Twitter
4. Solution: find a **proxy identity** those men publicly self-identify with
5. Attack the proxy → they show up in the comments to defend it → he now has a dense, free crowd of exactly his buyer, who have voluntarily raised their hands
6. Every post attaches the product to the specific insecurity the proxy implies

He never fought a person. He fought a *position*, and he picked the position because of **who would show up to defend it**.

That last line is the whole lesson. Your enemy isn't chosen by who annoys you. It's chosen by who defends it.

---

## The four gates before you fire a shot

Run every potential fight through these. If it fails one, don't start.

**Gate 1 — Can you name the transaction?**
Not "awareness." Not "engagement." A transaction. Tate's was: 15k in PhD sales that weekend.
- Bad: "I want dojo owners to know AI answering exists."
- Good: "40 booked audit calls in 14 days, 8 closed at $297/mo."
- Good: "600 school owners on my email list who've already told me their missed-call number."

**Gate 2 — Is the crowd that defends this position your buyer?**
Tate's crowd was his buyer. If you attack, say, Jiu-Jitsu belt politics, you'll get a huge fight and zero buyers, because the people who show up are hobbyist students, not school owners with a P&L.

**Gate 3 — Weight class.**
Tate's rule was 10k followers. Yours is different because your industry is small. The rule for you: **never attack a named individual school owner or a small competitor.** In a market of maybe 25,000 school owners in the US where everyone knows everyone through seminars and affiliations, a personal beef doesn't make you feared, it makes you unhireable. Attack practices, categories, and norms. Those have no feelings and can't retaliate.

**Gate 4 — What's the exit?**
Tate ran that thread over a weekend and stopped. Define yours before you start: a date, a number, or both. "I run this for 10 days or until I hit 40 booked audits, whichever comes first, then I go back to normal content." Without that, you become America in the Middle East — fighting a thing that used to be an asset and is now just your personality.

## Working backwards for your business

Your product: AI phone agent for martial arts schools.
Your buyer: the owner. Usually a black belt who loves teaching and hates admin, running $15k–60k/mo, who knows they're leaking leads but hasn't quantified it.

Now: **what position do these people publicly, emotionally defend — that correlates with them having a phone problem?**

Here's the answer, and it's your Star Wars:

> **"We're not a corporate gym. We're a family. We answer personally. The personal touch is what makes us different."**

That is the single most defended belief in the martial arts school world. It is also *the exact objection to your product*. And here's the beautiful part: **the loudest defenders of "personal touch" are the ones with the worst missed-call rates**, because they've made a virtue out of not having systems.

Attack "the personal touch" and the people who flood your comments to argue are, by definition, your buyer list.

## Your enemy shortlist, ranked

**1. "The personal touch" as currently practiced.** Highest emotional charge, perfect buyer overlap, no named target, zero retaliation risk.

**2. Martial arts marketing agencies selling ads into a broken phone.** Buyer overlap is excellent — anyone who's paid an agency has proven they'll spend money on growth and is probably already resentful. Attack the practice, not a named agency: "Your agency will never tell you your phone is the problem, because then you'd stop buying leads."

**3. The front desk hire.** A $2,400/mo part-timer who is on the mat or wiping down bags during the 5–8pm call window. Pure math argument, low heat, easy to win.

**4. Voicemail itself.** Broad, safe, slightly generic — good as a supporting drumbeat, weak as a main campaign.

**5. Gym management software** (the Zen Planners of the world). "Software that tracks the students you already have, not the ones you never got." Medium risk — some of them are potential integration partners. Don't burn this bridge unless you've decided you'll never partner.

## Campaign build: "Your Personal Touch Is a Voicemail Box"

**Objective:** 400 new emails + 40 booked missed-call audits in 14 days.
**Exit:** Day 14 or 40 audits.

**The proof asset.** Tate had cars and girls. Your equivalent is data you generate yourself: mystery-shop 50 martial arts schools in one metro area at 6:45pm on a Tuesday. Log who answers, who sends you to voicemail, who calls back and how fast. Publish the number.

This is a self-generating war machine. Nobody can argue with it, everybody feels accused, and every owner who reads it immediately wonders about their own number. That wondering is the sale.

**Sample shots:**

> Called 50 martial arts schools in Phoenix last Tuesday at 6:45pm. Posed as a parent asking about kids classes.
>
> 19 answered.
> 31 went to voicemail.
> 6 of the 31 ever called back.
> Fastest callback: 19 hours.
>
> Every one of those 50 websites says "family atmosphere."

> "We answer every call personally."
>
> You were on the mat. Your phone was in the office. It rang 4 times during the 5:30 kids class and you found out about it Thursday.
>
> That's not personal. That's just slow.

> Your competitor down the road isn't better at martial arts than you.
>
> He just picks up.

> A mom who calls three schools signs up at whichever one talks to her first. Not the best one. The first one.
>
> You've been losing to worse instructors for years and calling it market conditions.

> "Family atmosphere."
> "Personal attention."
> "We treat every student like our own."
>
> *press 1 for hours, press 2 to leave a message*

**The bridge.** This is where 95% of people fail. Tate put a link on *every single tweet*. The fight was never the content — the fight was the traffic source. You need one mechanism, repeated identically, on every post:

> Send me your school's number. I'll pull how many calls you missed last month and what those students were worth. Free, takes me 10 minutes, no pitch unless you ask.

That's your cobratate.com link. Same wording every time. Never a variation, never "DM me if you're interested," never a soft close.

> Agency: "We generated 60 leads this month!"
> You: "We signed 4."
> Agency: "Lead quality varies, let's optimize the creative."
>
> The creative is fine. Nobody answered the phone.

> Cheapest growth available to you right now, in order:
> 1. Answer the phone
> 2. Answer the phone faster
> 3. Follow up with the people who already called
> 4. Everything your agency sells you

**Proof asset:** take one client, show the before/after. Missed calls month one, missed calls month three, trials booked, students enrolled, revenue. One real school with real numbers beats fifty clever posts.

## Where Tate's context doesn't transfer, and what to do instead

Be clear-eyed about this, because copying him exactly will hurt you:

**His audience was millions of strangers with no repeat interaction. Yours is a village.** Your buyers see each other at seminars, share affiliations, and are in the same Facebook groups. Reputation compounds both ways. This means: attack positions relentlessly, attack people never. If a specific owner shows up angry in your comments, be gracious and helpful in public. You want onlookers thinking *this guy is right and he was cool about it*, not *this guy dunked on Sensei Mike*.

**His sale was a $50 impulse buy. Yours is a recurring B2B contract.** There is no "$15k over a weekend" outcome here. The realistic shape is: war generates attention in week one, audits get booked in weeks one to two, closes land in weeks two to six. Judge the campaign on booked audits, not on likes, and don't panic on day three when revenue hasn't moved.

**His provocation was funny.** That's the load-bearing part most imitators drop. The Star Wars posts worked because they were absurd enough to be entertaining — bystanders shared them *for the comedy*, which is what took it beyond the people he was insulting. Cruel without funny just makes you look bitter. If a shot isn't at least a little funny, cut it.

## The scoreboard

Before you launch anything, write these down:

- Transaction target (number + date)
- Exit condition
- One enemy position, written as a sentence
- One bridge, worded identically everywhere
- One proof asset that generates itself

If you can't fill all five, you don't have a war. You have a mood. And a mood posted publicly is just you paying attention costs with no revenue on the other side — which is exactly the endless-desert scenario he's warning about.

The core lesson in that passage is **not "pick fights online."** It's:

> **Attention is only valuable when it's connected to a deliberate business objective.**

Tate's example is essentially a marketing case study disguised as internet drama. Strip away the personality, insults, and specific products, and the framework becomes:

1. Identify a target audience.
2. Create a polarizing conversation that attracts that audience.
3. Connect the conversation to a specific offer.
4. Measure revenue, not engagement.
5. Exit when the objective is achieved.

For your business (**AI phone agents for martial arts schools**), the question becomes:

> "How do I create strategic attention that leads to martial arts school owners buying AI phone agents?"

# The Business Translation

If I condensed the entire lesson into one sentence for your AI phone agent business:

> **Never create attention unless you already know exactly how that attention will move a martial arts school owner closer to buying your AI phone agent.**

The "war" is not the point.

The strategy is the point.

For you, the most profitable version of this framework is usually not attacking people. It's creating strong, opinionated content around costly industry problems (missed calls, slow response times, lead leakage, front-desk overload) and then systematically connecting that attention to demos, audits, case studies, and sales conversations.
