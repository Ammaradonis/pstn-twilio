## The part of this that's real

Strip the delivery and there's one durable claim: **pick what you sell based on whether it sells, not on whether you like it.** That's correct, and most people get it backwards.

Here's what it means concretely inside AI phone agents for martial arts schools.

**"It doesn't matter what you sell, it matters that it sells."**

You already picked the vertical. The live version of this rule now operates *inside* it, and it mostly shows up as a segment question:

- If 200-student kids' taekwondo and karate schools buy at 3x the rate of hardcore adult BJJ academies, sell to the kids' schools. Even if you train BJJ. Even if the BJJ gym is where you'd rather hang out. The kids' school has a front desk, a real ad budget, 4-year student LTV, and a parent-driven phone line that rings all day. The 60-person adult gym has a broke owner who answers his own phone.
- If schools inside a franchise network (ATA, Gracie Barra, Premier Martial Arts) convert faster because one owner refers three others, chase franchise networks and stop cold-calling independents.
- If schools with 150+ students close in one call and schools with 40 students take four calls and then churn in month three, put a floor on your prospect list and let the small ones go.

**"Passionate for profit, not product."**

The trap for you is falling in love with the tech instead of the outcome. Examples of what that looks like in practice:

- You spend three weeks shaving voice latency from 900ms to 600ms. No school owner has ever mentioned latency. They mention "did it book the trial." Stop.
- You want to build a slick dashboard with analytics. Schools want a text message that says "Sarah booked Tuesday 4pm, kid is 7." Build the text.
- You want multi-language support because it's technically interesting. Zero customers asked. One customer asked four times for the agent to stop quoting prices. Build that instead.
- You resist selling a stripped-down $197/mo missed-call-text-back product because it's "not the real product." If it sells five times faster than the $497 full agent, it *is* the real product.

**"Bringing money in before I put money out."**

This is the strongest line in the whole passage and the easiest to implement:

- Pre-sell ten founding schools on annual prepay at a discount before you build the next feature set. Their money funds the build.
- Charge a setup fee ($500 to $1,500) that covers your onboarding time, so every new customer is cash-positive on day one instead of month five.
- Run the first accounts semi-manually if you have to. You or a VA handling overflow calls with a script is a fine version one. Sell the outcome, automate later.
- When a school asks for a custom feature, quote it, charge for it, build it, then sell it to everyone else as standard.
- Don't buy the logo, the office, the fancy domain, or the sales hire until 20 schools are paying.

## The part that will cost you money if you take it literally

> "You don't need to know about things to sell things, you need to know about selling to sell things."

This is roughly true for commodity consumer products. Nobody interviews the makeup brand's founder before buying a lipstick. It is substantially false for vertical B2B, which is what you're running.

Martial arts school owners are a small, tight, heavily-pitched community. They're in the same Facebook groups, the same association seminars, the same three software platforms. They've been burned by marketing agencies repeatedly. They will test you in the first thirty seconds, usually with "do you train?"

Concrete ways ignorance shows up and kills deals:

- **Vocabulary.** Your agent says "class" where the school says "open mat." Says "belt test" where they say "grading." Says "karate" to a taekwondo school. A parent hears it, the owner hears the recording, and you sound like a foreigner selling into their house.
- **The price question.** Most schools deliberately *refuse* to quote tuition on the phone. Their entire sales process is: get the kid in for a trial, sell in person. An outsider builds an agent that helpfully answers "our program is $189/month" and destroys the school's close rate. You just shipped a product that loses them students.
- **The intro offer.** "Two free classes" versus "$49 intro month with free uniform" versus "free trial week" are industry-standard variants and each school has a specific one. Your agent has to be scripted to their offer, not a generic booking flow.
- **Parent objections.** "Is it safe for a 5-year-old?" "Will he learn to fight other kids?" "What about sparring?" These come up on every call. An agent that fumbles them loses the booking.
- **Seasonality.** Enrollment spikes in late August/September and January. If you don't know that, you run your launch campaign in July, get nothing, and conclude the market is dead.

So take Tate's framing and keep it mercenary: **learn martial arts school operations not because you love it, but because domain fluency is a closing tool with measurable ROI.** Every hour you spend understanding how these schools actually sell raises your close rate and cuts your churn. That's not passion. That's research spend.

Cheap ways to buy that knowledge fast:

- Sit in on twenty real inbound calls at two friendly schools. Record them. Your scripts write themselves.
- Pay one school owner $500/month as an advisor and let him tear apart your agent.
- Read the school management software forums (Kicksite, Gymdesk, Zen Planner) to see what owners complain about.
- Go to one industry event (Martial Arts SuperShow, Mile High Karate summits). One weekend buys you the vocabulary and twelve warm leads.

## One honest caveat on the passage itself

The "I could sell a makeup brand tomorrow" claim is unfalsifiable and it's doing a lot of rhetorical work. Notice that the argument is built entirely on hypotheticals and an imagined Chinese concrete billionaire, not on a case of him actually entering an unfamiliar vertical and winning. Take the demand-first logic, which is sound. Don't take the confidence as evidence.

The other practical issue: vertical SaaS is a grind of support tickets, integrations, and churn calls. You don't need to love martial arts, but you do need to tolerate martial arts school owners for several years. Founders who find their customers tedious tend to quit around month fourteen, right when the compounding starts. That's not a moral point, it's a durability one.

## What to do this month

1. Rank your existing customers by revenue and churn risk. Find the segment that pays most and complains least. That's your ICP now, whatever you assumed before.
2. Kill the two features nobody has asked for in 60 days.
3. Pre-sell five annual prepays at a discount. Use the cash for the next build.
4. Rewrite your agent's script around the school's actual intro offer and their price-deflection rule, not a generic booking flow.
5. Book twenty call recordings from two schools and rebuild your objection handling from the transcripts.

# The most useful version of this lesson for your business

Don't interpret the passage as:

> "Money is all that matters."

Interpret it as:

> "Never become more committed to your product than your customer's result."

If an AI phone agent helps martial arts schools get more students, sell it aggressively.

If tomorrow something else helps them get more students better than an AI phone agent, be willing to sell that instead.

The founder who is passionate about AI may struggle.

The founder who is passionate about creating measurable business results—and getting paid for those results—is usually the one who builds the larger company.
