There's real sales skill buried in that transcript, mixed in with a con. If you copy the whole thing, the con part will sink a subscription business selling to martial arts school owners. So below I'll pull out the parts that work, show you what each looks like in your business, and then explain which parts to leave out and why.

## 1. Find out WHY they actually buy

**From the transcript:** "What you're gonna sell, WHY do people want it?" He says people don't buy Hustlers University for the videos. They buy it because they want to make money.

This is the most valuable idea in the whole piece. Nobody running a dojo wants an AI phone agent. They want what it gets them, and different owners want different things:

> **More students:** "I need 30 more members to stop stressing about rent."

> **Staying on the mat:** "I opened this school to teach, not to answer phones."

> **Getting their evenings back:** "I'm returning calls at 10pm instead of being with my kids."

> **Not paying a front desk salary:** "I can't afford a receptionist but I'm drowning without one."

> **Feeling like a real business:** "The big franchise across town looks professional. I look like a guy with a cell phone."

**Implement it:** On every sales call, ask "What made you look into this right now?" and then stop talking. Write their exact words down. After 20 calls, you'll have your ad copy, in their language.

## 2. Sell the result, not the product

**From the transcript:** "Fuck the product, who cares what's in the product? Sell the result." A protein shake ad listing carbs and sugar is boring. Being strong and fit is what sells.

Most AI phone agent companies list features, which is the "this much carbs, this much sugar" mistake:

| What most people say (the carbs and sugar) | What you should say (the result) |
|---|---|
| 24/7 AI call answering | Every parent who calls at 8pm gets booked into a trial class before they call the gym down the street |
| Natural-sounding voice | Callers don't hang up, and most don't realize it's AI |
| Calendar integration | You walk in Monday and three trial classes are already on your schedule |
| Answers FAQs about pricing and schedule | You stop answering "how much is it?" twelve times a day |
| Call summaries and transcripts | You know exactly who called, what they wanted, and who to follow up with |

Features still belong on your website. They just come after the result, as proof it works.

## 3. Point at the pain they're already in

**From the transcript:** "Why are you suffering in the gym for no reason?" He takes a problem the customer already feels and makes it vivid.

This works when the pain is real and you describe it accurately. For school owners, it's very real:

> "You teach the 5:30 kids class. That's exactly when parents get off work and call. Who's answering?"

> "A missed call isn't a missed call. It's a student who might have trained with you for three years."

> "When a parent's voicemail goes unanswered, they don't wait. They call the next school on Google."

> "You're a black belt instructor spending your best hours as an unpaid receptionist."

Do the math with them, using their own numbers. Something like: "If you miss 10 calls from prospects a week, and a third of those would have booked a trial, and half of those trials sign up at your monthly rate, what's that per year?" When they calculate it themselves, they believe it far more than any number you give them.

Better still, offer a free missed-call audit. Many phone systems can show missed calls, or you can have them forward calls to your agent for a week. Showing an owner that they missed 23 calls last week beats any pitch you could write.

## 4. Speak to identity and status

**From the transcript:** Porsche and Mercedes ads show "Mr. Businessman," important and successful, and the buyer wants to be him.

Martial arts owners care deeply about identity: instructor, leader, role model, someone who built something. Show them the version of themselves they want to become:

> "Run your school like the ones with 400 students."

> "Stay on the mat. Your phone handles itself."

> "The schools that grow aren't better at karate. They answer the phone."

> "Be the instructor, not the receptionist."

**Here's where to diverge from Tate:** He adds "you're a nobody, you're a loser." For your market, that's poison. Martial arts culture is built on respect, and owners are proud people who've trained for decades. Criticize their situation, not them. "Your phone setup is costing you students" makes them want to fix it. "You're a loser for missing calls" makes them hang up on a stranger who insulted them.

## 5. Tailor the pitch to the person in front of you

**From the transcript:** The cam girl example. When a fat old man asks what she likes, she describes someone mature, not a young fit Italian, so he can see himself in it.

The skill in that example is useful: listen to who you're talking to and emphasize what matters to them. It's standard sales discovery. What made it a con is that she was lying. You can do the useful part honestly:

> **Solo owner teaching every class:** Talk about being stuck on the mat while calls go to voicemail, and the stress of doing everything alone.

> **Multi-location owner:** Talk about consistency, so every location answers the same way and they can see every lead in one place.

> **Kids-focused karate school:** Talk about parents who call in the evening and after school, the times the owner is busiest.

> **Competition BJJ gym:** Talk about the head coach's training time, and not losing adult prospects who call once and never call back.

Your AI agent should do this for callers too. It can ask, "What got you interested in classes?" and respond based on the answer, as long as everything it says is true about that school:

> **Parent worried about bullying:** The agent talks about the kids program's focus on confidence and books a trial.

> **Adult wanting fitness:** The agent talks about the adult program and beginner-friendly classes.

> **Someone who trained years ago:** The agent mentions returning students and how classes are organized by rank.

That will convert better than a generic "Our classes are Monday through Thursday."

## What to leave out: the con

In the cam girl model, the guy is told what he wants to hear, including "maybe one day we'll meet," and he pays for two years for something that was never going to happen. It works only because the customer can't find out he's being lied to. There's also no nice way to say it: it's taking money from lonely people under false pretenses. It's not a business model worth copying.

It also won't work for you, for practical reasons:

**Your customers find out in weeks, not years.** Either the agent books trial classes or it doesn't. If you overpromise, the owner sees it in the first month.

**You sell a subscription.** Your profit comes from customers staying for years. Every exaggeration in your pitch becomes cancellations and refund requests.

**The martial arts world is small.** Owners talk at seminars, in Facebook groups, and through associations and franchises. One burned owner can close doors you didn't know existed.

**Misleading advertising is regulated.** In Austria and the EU, misleading claims are covered by unfair-competition law even when selling business to business. I'm not a lawyer, but "guaranteed 50 new students" with nothing behind it is exactly the kind of claim that invites problems.

Tate's "PROMISE everything's gonna be better" works better as **proof and guarantees**:

> **Proof:** Play prospects a real recording of your agent booking a trial class.

> **Proof:** Show a before/after from a real client, such as missed calls per week before and after.

> **Guarantee:** "If the agent doesn't book at least X trial classes in your first 60 days, you don't pay." Only offer this if you've set a number you can reliably hit.

> **Low-risk start:** "Try it on your after-hours calls only for two weeks."

## Putting it together

Here's a short outreach message that uses every principle above honestly:

> "Quick question: who answers your phone during the 5:30 class?
>
> For most school owners, the answer is voicemail. And parents who hit voicemail usually just call the next school on Google.
>
> We set up an AI receptionist that answers every call, answers questions about your programs, and books trial classes straight into your schedule, even at 9pm on a Sunday.
>
> Want me to run a free one-week missed-call check so you can see how many calls you're actually losing?"

It speaks to the real reason they'd buy (students), names a pain that's actually happening (the 5:30 class), sells the result instead of features, respects the owner, and ends with proof instead of a promise.

The passage is advocating a specific persuasion model:

1. Identify a painful insecurity, frustration, fear, or desire.
2. Make that pain emotionally vivid.
3. Position your product as the bridge to the desired outcome.
4. Keep attention on the outcome, not the technical details.
5. Repeat the connection consistently.

However, there's an important distinction between:

* **Manipulating insecurities** ("You're a loser without this.")
* **Addressing existing frustrations and aspirations** ("You're working hard and want better results. Here's a way to get them.")

The second approach is usually more sustainable, ethical, and effective for legitimate businesses.

For your AI phone agent business serving martial arts schools, the equivalent is not "make prospects feel bad." It's understanding the deep frustrations school owners already have and speaking directly to them.
