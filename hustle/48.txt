## First, strip it down

Underneath the theatrics there are three actual mechanisms, and they're separable. Most people who "internalize" this stuff copy the swagger and miss the machinery.

1. **Never refuse money over the *mechanics* of how it arrives.** Currency, rail, timing, form.
2. **Never refuse a job because you don't currently know how to do it.** Price it, take the money, then solve it or broker it.
3. **Build a golden bridge.** Actively remove every step between "I want this" and "you have my money."

The roof tiles story is the whole lesson in one image. Sebastian didn't get better at sales. He got better at *accepting*. The deal was already won and he almost lost it at the payment step.

Here's each one in your business.

---

## 1. Never refuse money over mechanics

A dojo owner has decided to buy. Then your checkout kills it. Count how many ways you currently make that possible.

**Rail and instrument:**
- Card only, no SEPA/ACH? A 200-student karate school in Germany runs everything off bank transfer. They're not entering a card.
- No PayPal? A lot of small US gyms live on it.
- No crypto? Some of your best-fit buyers, the young BJJ gym owners, hold it and would rather spend it than euros.
- No cash? A guy at a seminar in Bucharest pulls out €800 and wants to start Monday. Take it, write a receipt, book it as revenue.
- No invoice-and-transfer option? Franchise-owned schools (ATA, Tiger Schulmann, Gracie Barra affiliates) often *can't* pay by card, they need a PO and an invoice with terms.

**Timing:**
- Annual only? Dead on arrival for a school clearing €4k/month.
- Monthly only? You're leaving money on the table with the school that would happily pay 10 months up front for two free.
- Can't do quarterly? Martial arts is brutally seasonal. January and September are flush, July is a graveyard. Offer a payment schedule that's heavy in Jan/Sep and light in summer and you'll close owners who'd otherwise say "not right now."
- No deferred start? "Pay nothing for 30 days, then €X" closes the risk-averse owner who's been burned by a website agency.

**Structure:**
- Flat SaaS fee only? Many owners won't add a fifth subscription. Offer **€25 per booked trial** instead. Same money, completely different psychological category. It's marketing spend, not software.
- No rev-share option? "€0 up front, 50% of the first month's membership fee for every student the agent enrolls" will close owners who will never sign a contract.
- No multi-location bundle? An owner with three locations under one LLC wants one invoice, not three.
- No down-market product? If your only price is €399/month, every school below that threshold is a hard no. A €49 "after-hours only" tier converts them and upgrades later.

**The rule:** the only acceptable reason to lose a deal is that they don't want it. Losing it because of your Stripe configuration is self-harm.

## 2. Never refuse a job you don't currently do

This is the "tigers tamed" mechanic, and for you it's the highest-leverage one, because every custom yes in a software business either becomes a product feature or a margin.

**Integration asks:**
- "Does it work with Kicksite?" You don't have that integration. Correct answer: "Yes, €1,500 setup and it's live in three weeks." Now you've been paid to build an integration that makes every other Kicksite school in the world addressable. You got a customer to fund your roadmap.
- Same for Gymdesk, PushPress, Zen Planner, Martialytics, Spark Membership. Each one is a locked market that one customer will pay you to unlock.
- "Can it push into our Google Sheet instead?" Yes. Zapier. Twenty minutes.

**Scope asks:**
- "Can it also send an SMS follow-up if they don't show for the trial?" Yes, price it.
- "Can it handle Turkish?" You're in Austria, this will come up. Yes. Price it, test it, and now you've got a wedge into every Turkish-owned gym in Vienna.
- "Can it call our 400 dead leads from last year?" That's an outbound campaign, not your product. Say yes, quote €2,000, run it. You just found a second product line with a much easier sale.
- "Can you rebuild our website?" You don't do websites. Quote €3,500, pay a freelancer €1,800, keep the rest. You didn't touch a line of code.
- "Can you run our Meta ads?" Broker it. Find one good martial-arts-specific media buyer, mark up 40%, own the relationship.
- "Can you do this for my wife's dance studio?" Yes. The product is identical. So is yoga, so is tutoring, so is a driving school.

**The discipline Tate skips, and it matters in your model:** his examples are one-shot transactions. You have recurring support costs. So the rule isn't "say yes to everything." It's **say yes at a price that funds the solution, take the money before you build, and convert every custom yes into either a repeatable feature or a bounded one-off engagement with an end date.** Otherwise you accumulate twelve bespoke Frankenstein deployments and your support load eats the company by month nine.

## 3. Never refuse money over *what* it is

The roof tiles. A martial arts school is asset-poor in cash and surprisingly asset-rich in things you actually need.

What they can pay you in:

- **A tournament booth.** A regional BJJ or karate tournament puts 40 gym owners in one gymnasium for a weekend. A school that's an organizer can hand you a table. That's worth more than their €400/month.
- **An introduction to their association.** A Shotokan school inside a 60-dojo federation. Trade three free months for a warm intro and a slot at the annual instructor seminar. That's not a customer, that's a channel.
- **Content.** They have mats, good light, photogenic students, and a camera. Trade a discount for the right to film a proper case-study video on their floor. Buying that production costs you €2,000.
- **A speaking slot.** Their instructor camp, their black belt grading weekend, their affiliate call. You get twenty minutes in front of exactly your buyer.
- **Their software rep.** They know their Gymdesk account manager. That intro is a partnership conversation you'd otherwise cold-email into for months.
- **Testimonial and referral rights, contractually.** "Half price, and you introduce me to four owners you know by name, on the phone, not by email." That's the roof-tile trade. You just have to know what you'll do with the tiles.
- **Equipment.** Gis, focus mitts, a mat order. Only take it if you have a buyer, the same way Sebastian already knew who to call. Otherwise you own a garage full of gis.
- **Training.** Cheap for them, genuinely valuable, and it embeds you in the community you sell to.

The discipline: **know your exit before you accept.** Sebastian could take roof tiles because he had a guy. Take a tournament booth only if you'll work it. Take equipment only if you know who's buying. Barter without an exit is just a discount you gave yourself.

---

## 4. The golden bridge, made concrete

Audit every step between wanting and paying. For each one, ask what happens if you delete it.

- Can a school owner buy at 10pm on a Sunday without talking to you? If no, you're losing the impulse buyers.
- Does your proposal have a live payment link in it, or does it have "let me know and I'll send an invoice"? That gap is where deals die.
- Can you close on the demo call? Payment link in chat, signed and paid before they hang up. Every day between demo and payment cuts close rate.
- Is your contract three pages a gym owner can read, or eleven pages that makes him think he needs a lawyer?
- Is there a price for a guy who only has €50? There should always be a door.
- Do you require them to cancel their existing system first? Never. Run parallel.

## 5. Where this is wrong, and it'll cost you

Two places, and I'd be doing you a disservice to skip them.

**Fit is not mechanics.** "Never say no to money" holds completely for *how* someone pays. It does not hold for *who*. A school with 30 students getting six calls a week will get no measurable ROI, churn in 60 days after you've burned onboarding cost, and then tell people it didn't work. Martial arts is a small, gossipy world — everyone sees everyone at gradings and tournaments. One bad reference from a school that was never going to succeed costs you more than the €400 you took. Qualify on call volume and student count. Say no, and say it as "you're not ready for this yet, come back at 80 students," which makes you the guy with standards.

**"Get rich before you get legal" is the line that's genuinely expensive for you specifically.** Not on principle. On arithmetic:

- Barter is taxable income at fair market value. Roof tiles are revenue. Austria treats them as revenue. Recording them properly costs you an afternoon; not recording them costs you back taxes plus interest plus penalties.
- Cross-border VAT on B2B SaaS (reverse charge inside the EU, different treatment for US customers) is the kind of thing that quietly accrues into a five-figure bill.
- Your product records phone calls, in Europe, frequently with parents calling about children's classes. GDPR isn't optional decoration here, and the EU AI Act's transparency requirement that callers be told they're speaking with an AI is now in force. Worth confirming the current specifics with someone qualified, but the direction of travel is certain.
- More to the point commercially: every serious school, every franchise, every association will ask about this before signing. Being the vendor with a clean DPA and a compliant disclosure isn't a cost centre. It's how you win the 200-student accounts while your competitors are stuck selling to hobbyists.

Take money in any form. Just book it.

## 6. Your card

The card isn't a joke because it's a positioning device. It says: *don't shop me by feature, tell me the outcome and I'll price it.* Yours might read:

> **The phone never goes unanswered again.**
> Missed calls returned in 30 seconds · Trials booked at 2am · Price questions handled · Kids' class enquiries · No-show follow-up · Dead lead resurrection · Reactivation campaigns · Any software, any language, any hour
>
> *If it happens on your phone, we handle it. Tell us what you need and we'll quote it.*

Every line is a thing a school owner has lost sleep over, and the last line is the Tate mechanic: the question isn't whether you can, it's what it costs.

The useful core of that rant is **not** "do anything for money" and definitely not the illegal parts (revolutions, assassinations, ignoring laws, etc.). The business lesson hiding underneath all the theatrics is:

> **"Remove friction between a prospect wanting to buy and you getting paid."**

and

> **"Don't limit yourself to only selling what you personally know how to do. Sell outcomes."**

For an AI phone agent business serving martial arts schools, that's actually a powerful framework when translated correctly.

# Principle 1: Never Say No to Revenue Opportunities

What Tate is really saying:

> If someone wants to buy something valuable from you, don't immediately reject the opportunity because it doesn't fit your current offer.

Most agency owners hear a request and think:

* "We don't do that."
* "That's not our service."
* "I'm not an expert in that."
* "That's outside scope."

Instead think:

* "Can I solve this?"
* "Can I partner with someone?"
* "Can I package this?"
* "Can I refer and profit?"

# Principle 5: Stop Thinking Like a Technician

Many AI agency owners think:

> "Can I technically build this?"

Business owners think:

> "Can I profitably solve this?"

Those are different questions.

## Example

School asks:

> "Can you build a system that answers calls, books trials, follows up by text, and reactivates old students?"

> "That's complicated."

* $200 average monthly membership

That's potentially thousands in recurring revenue for them.

Now you're discussing value, not complexity.
