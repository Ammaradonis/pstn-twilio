## Translating the vocabulary into your business

Strip out the slang and Tate is describing a three-stage pipeline plus a warning.

**The geese** are every martial arts school in your market: names on a list, websites, Google Maps pins. **Femeusing** is turning one of those anonymous schools into a specific person you actually know: the owner's name, how many students they have, when their phone goes unanswered. Once you know them, that school is **a goose**. **Famousing** is turning that goose into money in your bank, now rather than "in the new year." The warning, **don't get famoused**, means don't let anyone do the same thing to you: waste your time or take your money on promises they never intend to keep.

## 1. Femeuse the geese: turn the list into people

Tate says the geese become a goose "once you start interacting with them and you get to know them a bit more personal." At T2 that meant calling company after company until someone like Glenda became a real person with a real budget. You have an advantage he didn't: you can prove the pain before you ever speak to the owner.

**Mystery-call the school during class time.** Most instructors are on the mat at 6pm and can't answer. Call, get voicemail, and note the time. Your opener becomes: "I called your school at 6:40 last Tuesday and nobody picked up. I'm guessing that was during your kids' class?" That school has stopped being a pin on a map. You now know something specific about their problem.

**Do the maths on their missed calls before the meeting.** Look up their trial offer and tuition on their website. Then ask: "If one missed call a week is a parent who would have booked a trial, and a third of trials become members, what's a member worth to you over a year?" Let them say the number. That turns the owner from "a school" into "a school losing roughly X a month," which is exactly the knowledge Tate means by getting personal.

**Go where the geese flock.** Local tournaments, belt gradings, seminars, and school-owner Facebook groups are all places where an anonymous list becomes faces and names. One conversation at a tournament is worth fifty cold emails.

**Ask every client for their friends.** School owners know other owners through federations, affiliations, and shared instructors. A referral arrives already half-femeused, because someone they trust vouched for you.

## 2. Famous the goose: close now, not "later"

This is the Glenda story, and the key move is how Tate responds when she says "probably in the new year." He doesn't reply "okay, I'll call you then." He agrees with her timeline and uses it as the reason to start today: if you want to be on TV in spring, the paperwork has to happen now so you don't miss the window.

Your version is almost identical, because an AI agent has real setup time. It has to learn their class schedule, pricing, trial booking process, and FAQs, connect to their booking software, and get test-called and tuned before it's ready.

**The "after the busy season" objection:**

> Owner: "Sounds interesting, let's talk after New Year."
> You: "Perfect, January's your biggest enrollment month, so that's exactly when this needs to be flawless. When do you want it answering calls? … Right, so if we want it live and tested before the resolution rush, we need to start building your scripts now. The last thing I want is your agent going live untested in your busiest week of the year."

It doesn't matter whether they say January or March. Like Glenda's answer, the date just sets the deadline you work back from.

**The "I need to talk to my partner" objection (Glenda's husband):**

Tate doesn't argue. He asks for a commitment plus a partial payment. Your version: "Of course, talk to them. What I need today is a commitment so I can reserve your setup slot and start building. We take the setup fee now, and your monthly doesn't start until you're live." Even better, book the three-way call on the spot: "Is your partner free tomorrow at 7? Let's do fifteen minutes together."

**The demo close.** At T2 they tried "every hard close," including the creative close of showing clients what their ad would look like. Your equivalent is to build a rough demo agent from their website before the meeting. Then hand them your phone and have them call it: "Ask it what time the kids' class is on Thursday." Owners who hear their own school's name answered by a working agent stop saying "later."

**The early-commitment discount.** Tate's pitch included "if you buy now, you get a special discount on next year." For you, that could be a lower setup fee or a free month for annual prepay signed this month. Only do this if you'd genuinely honor it for anyone who signs this month.

## 3. Money in their bank isn't money you can spend

This is the point of the cam girl story, and it applies directly to your pipeline. Her customers said "I'm saving for your birthday," then spent the money on other girls in the meantime. On the day, they tipped less than they would have spent anyway over those months. A verbal "yes, in January" from a school owner behaves the same way.

**The competitor gets there first.** The owner who is "definitely signing in January" meets another AI receptionist company at a seminar in November. Tate's version: "over Christmas we decided that's not for us."

**The budget gets spent elsewhere.** "We'll start after the tournament" turns into "we spent the budget on new mats and the tournament." The money they were saving for you went somewhere else.

**The DIY alternative.** A student's cousin offers to "build them a chatbot," or they hire a part-time front desk person. The demand was real, but it didn't stay yours.

**The delay costs both of you.** This is the cumulative point Tate makes with the birthday tips. An owner who starts in October instead of January gives you three extra months of recurring revenue. They also get three months of calls answered that would otherwise have gone to voicemail. That's a true reason to start now, and it's in their interest as much as yours.

The practical rule: in your pipeline, verbal yeses count as zero. Only signed-and-paid counts as famoused.

## 4. Don't get famoused

Tate's warning is not to let someone "bullshit you and either take your money or take your time." In your niche, the common versions look like this:

**The endless free trial.** "Let us try it free for a month and we'll see." They use it, never pay, and you've done a full setup for nothing. The fix is a paid pilot or a setup fee up front.

**Custom-feature bait.** "If it integrates with our software and handles Spanish-speaking parents, I'll sign." You build it, and they vanish. Custom work gets paid before it gets built.

**"Send me a proposal."** Often this is a polite exit, not interest. Don't spend three hours on a deck. Ask one question first: "Happy to. Before I do, if the proposal matches what we discussed, is there anything stopping you from starting this month?"

**People selling to you.** Lead-gen agencies, "growth partners," and resellers who promise fifty schools if you just give them a cut or build them a white-label version first. You're the goose in that conversation.

**Late payers.** Put a card on file, bill automatically, and pause service on non-payment. Don't let a client hold your money in their bank.

## Where I'd part ways with the original

Two things in that transcript would hurt your business if you copied them.

First, the Glenda close worked because the urgency was real. The paperwork genuinely took time. If you invent deadlines ("price doubles Friday" when it doesn't), it will backfire in a niche this small, where owners compare notes in the same groups and federations. Your business also runs on subscriptions, not one-off invoices. A goose you pressure into signing who isn't a good fit will cancel in month two and tell other owners. In recurring revenue, the close is the start of the relationship, not the end. Famous the schools that actually miss calls and will actually benefit.

Second, the contempt in the cam girl section, both toward her and toward her customers, isn't part of the lesson, so I've left it out. The useful takeaway is "don't let demand sit in someone else's pocket." Treating people as marks is a different mindset, and school owners can smell it.

## Making it a daily habit

Tate's question, "where's the goose?", works well as a daily check. Each morning, ask three things. Which schools on my list am I going to turn into real people today? Which owners do I already know who haven't paid yet, and what's stopping me from asking for the commitment today? And who is currently taking my time without any sign they'll pay?

If you keep a simple pipeline with three columns (list, known owner, signed and paid), the whole lesson comes down to moving names right as fast as honestly possible, and never counting anything in the first two columns as money.

The core idea behind Tate's invented language is actually simple:

* **"Femeuse the geese"** = turn strangers into known prospects.
* **"Famous the goose"** = convert a specific prospect into a commitment now.
* **"Don't become a goose"** = don't be the one getting strung along, manipulated, or trapped in endless future promises.

Strip away the stories, insults, and jargon, and it's really a lesson about:

1. Pipeline creation.
2. Conversion.
3. Urgency.
4. Not confusing interest with commitment.

For your AI phone agents business targeting martial arts schools, this framework is extremely applicable.
