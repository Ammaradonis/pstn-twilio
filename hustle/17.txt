The core of this is sound business advice: attention you earn is cheaper than attention you buy, and attention is worthless unless you point it at a sale. A couple of parts will hurt you if you copy them literally, though, because Tate sells himself to millions of young men, and you sell trust to a small pool of martial arts school owners. I'll cover each principle, tie it back to the text, and flag what to change.

---

### 1. Earned attention costs time, not money

**What Tate said:** Buying attention through ads works but is expensive. Getting it for free is cheaper, but "it's still gonna take time."

**For you:** Ads aimed at school owners are pricey and cold. Your real budget is your time and creativity.

- **Mystery-shop study.** Personally call 100 schools at 7–8pm on weeknights and record how many go to voicemail, have full mailboxes, or never call back. That costs a few evenings and gives you material for a post, a cold email opener, a trade show slide, and a podcast pitch.
- **Missed-call math content.** Make short videos showing how one missed call from a parent turns into a lost multi-year student, and let owners plug in their own tuition. It's free to make and it makes your product's value obvious.
- **Show up in owner communities daily.** Answer questions in martial arts business Facebook groups about front desk problems, lead follow-up, and trial conversions. Most of the time, don't pitch. People will click your profile.
- **Pitch martial arts business podcasts** with a spicy angle like "Why most schools lose their best leads after 6pm." Hosts want guests with a take.

### 2. Controversy works, but aim it at a problem, not your buyers

**What Tate said:** He posted something that "pissed off the nerds," and the angry replies made it go viral.

**The detail most people miss:** Star Wars fans weren't the people he needed to win over, so he could afford to insult them. You can't insult school owners, because they *are* your market, and there aren't millions of them. So pick a fight with an enemy that owners already resent, or with a costly belief some of them hold. Owners who argue in the comments still spread your post and often come around later.

Hooks in that spirit:

- "Voicemail is where new students go to die."
- "'If they're serious, they'll call back' is the most expensive sentence in martial arts."
- "You don't have a lead problem. You have a phone problem."
- "Your front desk is you, on the mat, with your phone ringing in the office."
- "A wall full of black belts doesn't enroll the kid whose mom couldn't reach you."

### 3. Get attention, then sell shamelessly

**What Tate said:** Most people get attention and never monetize it. Every tweet after his hook pushed his website.

Here's his Star Wars thread rebuilt for your business. Fill the brackets with real numbers from your own mystery-shop calls, never invented ones:

> **Post 1:** Unpopular opinion: most martial arts schools don't need more leads. They need to answer the phone. A mom calls at 6:40pm. You're teaching. She calls the next school on Google. You paid for that lead and handed it to your competitor.
>
> **Post 2:** I called [X] schools at 7pm last week. [Y] went to voicemail. [Z] had full mailboxes. Here's what a parent hears when they call a school using [your company] at the same time: [recording]. Try it yourself: [demo number]
>
> **Post 3:** Every unanswered call is a kid who signs up somewhere else. Call our demo line and pretend you're a nervous parent. Try to stump it. [demo number]

Other ways to turn attention into sales:

- **One call to action everywhere.** Every post, video, and comment points to a single next step, ideally the demo number, because hearing the agent is what sells it.
- **The free missed-call audit.** "DM me your school's number and I'll call it tonight after hours and tell you what happened." Whatever the result, they've just felt the problem.
- **When something takes off,** pin a comment with the demo line and reply to the top comments. Tate kept selling in every follow-up, and you should too.

### 4. Paint your wall: make the product itself the attention-getter

**What Tate said:** The car-wrap guy's $3,000 mural paid for itself because it showed his craft to everyone who drove past. "Is your sign big and bright and bold enough?"

**For you:** Your mural is the AI talking. Nothing you write about it will be as persuasive as someone hearing it handle a real call.

- **Make the demo number your billboard.** Put it on your website header, email signature, business cards, and trade show booth: "Call now. Pretend you're a parent."
- **Let the agent answer your own business line.** Every prospect who calls you experiences the product before you say a word.
- **Show off timestamps.** Share (with permission) a screenshot or recording of a trial class booked at 11:47pm on a Sunday. The time stamp is the whole pitch.
- **Build a proof wall** as clients go live: "Trial booked 10:32pm," "Rescheduled 6:15am," "Answered during the black belt test."
- **Check your sign.** Can a stranger tell what you do within three seconds of landing on your site or profile? "24/7 phone answering built for martial arts schools" beats "AI-powered voice solutions."

### 5. The dinosaur marathon: do something unconventional and physical

**What Tate said:** "If you have to go and run a marathon dressed as a fuckin dinosaur with your company name on the back, then go do it."

- **Sponsor local and regional tournaments.** A kids' division costs little, and every owner and parent sees your name on the banner.
- **Start a white belt journey series.** Train at a school, document your progress, and talk about the business side as you go. A vendor who actually trains earns respect from owners fast.
- **Send lumpy mail.** Mail a toy phone to 50 dream schools with a note: "How many calls went to voicemail last month? Call this number to find out what you're missing."
- **Give memorable client gifts,** like a custom belt embroidered with the school's name and "Never Missed a Call." It ends up on the office wall where other owners see it.
- **Stand out at trade shows.** Show up in a gi with your brand on the back, and make the booth one big phone people are dared to call.

### 6. Study who already has the attention

**What Tate said:** Ask "Does my company get enough attention?" Then find out who in your industry does get it, and how.

Run his exact sequence:

- **List who owners listen to:** the martial arts business coaches, membership software companies, lead-gen agencies, and school owners with big Instagram followings.
- **For each, figure out the channel:** a podcast, a free Facebook group, a conference stage, a YouTube channel, a mastermind.
- **Borrow their audience instead of competing with it.** Lead-gen agencies are a great example: their results look better when leads actually get answered, so offer them a referral or partnership deal. Guest on the coaches' podcasts. Speak at their events.

### 7. Stand out in a sea of sameness

**What Tate said:** With 100,000 cam performers online looking basically identical, the ones who won differentiated through costumes, thumbnails, and interesting rooms.

**For you:** Owners are about to be flooded with generic "AI receptionist" pitches that all sound the same. Your edge is that you only serve martial arts.

- **Speak the language.** Demo the agent handling belt ranks, trial class versus intro lesson, kids versus adult programs, and questions like "My son is really shy, will he be okay?" A generic competitor can't do that.
- **Your thumbnail** is your cold email subject line, the first line of a DM, and the first two seconds of a video. "I called your school at 8:14pm last night" (only when true) gets opened. "Revolutionize your front desk with AI" doesn't.
- **Your room** is your website and profiles. Show mats, belts, and real schools, not stock images of blue robot brains.
- **Brand the agent to each school,** so callers hear "Thanks for calling Tiger Rock Karate," with a name and tone that fit the dojo.

### 8. Keep attention by making people feel remembered

**What Tate said:** The cam performers remembered customers' names and pets and greeted them when they arrived, so customers felt valued and kept coming back.

This applies to you on two levels.

- **You with your clients.** Remember owners' names and their kids. Congratulate them when a student wins a tournament or when they open a second location. Mark their school anniversary. Owners talk to each other at seminars and events, and the vendor who makes them feel seen gets referred.
- **Your product with their callers.** This is exactly what your AI can sell: greeting returning callers by name, remembering the child's name and which program the parent asked about, and following up. When you pitch, you can literally say, "Your agent will remember that Maria's son Lucas is 7 and wanted the Tuesday class." That's the loyalty principle turned into a feature.

### 9. Attention only counts if it becomes Money In

**What Tate said:** Cash flow is your only real business, and attention is what increases the number of sales coming in.

- **Track the metrics that pay you:** demo line calls, audits requested, demos booked, and clients signed. Likes and views don't count.
- **Give each tactic a deadline.** If it gets engagement but zero demo calls after a few weeks, cut it or change the call to action.
- **Label everything:** a different demo number or link for trade shows, Facebook groups, and cold email, so you know which attention actually converts.

### What not to copy

**"There's no such thing as bad attention."** This holds for Tate because his product *is* his persona, and his audience enjoys the edge. For you, it's false. Many of your buyers run kids' programs where parents' trust is everything, and they won't put a vendor on their phone line if that vendor is known for trashy or insulting content. Get kicked out of the two or three Facebook groups where owners gather and you've lost a real share of your market. In a small B2B market, bad attention is expensive.

**Flexing cars and women.** Flex proof instead: booked trials, client results, recordings, and timestamps. Owners care whether you can fill their classes, not what you drive.

**Exaggerated claims.** "Everyone who watched Star Wars is poor" works as an obvious joke. Business statistics have to be real, because owners will check with each other.

**Legal traps.** Get consent before sharing call recordings, since recording laws vary by location. Do your mystery-shop calls yourself as a human, not with the AI: outbound AI-voice calls are restricted in many places (in the US, robocall rules cover them), and GDPR applies in the EU. Check the rules wherever you sell.

### How to internalize it

Make it a weekly habit. Every Monday, ask yourself three questions:

1. What's my one attention bet this week (a hook, a stunt, a study, an event)?
2. Where is the sell in it, and what exact action do I want people to take?
3. Which of last week's bets produced demo calls, and which only produced likes?

Do that for three months, and "attention first, then sell" will stop being a quote you read and become how you think about every piece of marketing you put out.

The core idea in that text is **not** "be controversial." The deeper principle is:

> Attention precedes sales.
> If nobody notices you, your offer doesn't exist in the market.

The mistake many people make is copying the surface-level tactic (provocation, outrage, flexing) instead of understanding the underlying mechanism:

1. Capture attention.
2. Direct attention toward your offer.
3. Convert attention into revenue.
4. Repeat consistently.
