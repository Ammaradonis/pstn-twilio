## The part that's real

The core claim holds: "I can't afford it" is almost always a ranking problem, not a balance problem. A school owner who tells you they have no money is still paying $600/month for Facebook ads, $150 for Zen Planner or Kicksite, $4,000+ in rent, and probably $1,800/month to a part-time front desk person who works 20 hours a week. Money is moving. It's just moving somewhere else.

That reframe is worth internalizing because of what it does to *you*, not to them. If you believe the prospect is broke, you stop selling and start apologizing. You discount before you're asked. You say "no worries, reach out when things pick up" and hang up on a live deal. Most lost sales in your category die from the seller's belief, not the buyer's wallet.

## The part that will cost you money if you take it literally

Three things in that transcript don't transfer to what you sell.

**"Your offer has to be more important than food."** For a consumer info product sold to individuals, sure. You sell B2B. A dojo owner isn't choosing between your phone agent and dinner. He's choosing between your phone agent and a new set of mats, another $500/month in ads, a second instructor, or the sparring gear his students keep asking about. Competing against food is a fantasy. Competing against his *next business purchase* is the actual fight, and it's a fight you can win with arithmetic instead of pressure.

**The vice examples.** Cam sites and gambling survive recessions because of compulsion, not value. You cannot engineer compulsion into B2B software, and trying to is what produces the desperate closer who gets blocked on Facebook. Your product wins on a different mechanism entirely: it's a multiplier on money the school is already spending.

**"Who gives a shit what, find a way around the objection."** This is the line that will actually damage you. Martial arts is a small, incestuous industry. Owners are in the same Facebook groups, the same association seminars, the same Zoom masterminds. They talk. If you push a school with genuinely bad cash flow into a contract and it churns in 60 days with a chargeback and a complaint post, you don't lose one customer, you lose a referral network. Some schools *are* genuinely underwater. Not many, but some. The move isn't to pretend they don't exist, it's to disqualify them fast and spend your hours on the 90% who are simply mis-ranking you.

## The reframe that actually applies to you

Don't position against food. Position against **money they're already burning**.

Your product's real category is not "software." It's "recovery of leads already paid for." Every school owner has a bucket with a hole in it. You don't sell a bucket. You sell the patch, priced at a fraction of what leaks out.

The whole sale collapses into one sentence: *You are already paying for these leads. You are not answering the phone when they call. Here is what that costs you per month.*

## The arithmetic you need memorized

Learn these numbers cold for the specific school you're talking to. Ask for them in discovery. Don't guess.

- Monthly ad spend
- Cost per lead
- Trial-to-enrollment rate
- Average monthly tuition
- Average student lifespan in months (this gives you LTV)
- Rough missed-call volume

Then the math runs itself. Say a school spends $800/month on ads at $40 a lead, so 20 leads. Tuition is $170/month, average student stays 11 months, so LTV is roughly $1,870. He closes maybe a third of trials. If he's missing even 6 calls a month that would have booked a trial, and two of those would have enrolled, that's around $3,700 in lifetime revenue walking to the school four miles away. Every month. Against a product priced at, what, $300-500/month?

Run that on a screen share while he watches. Use *his* numbers, not yours. The moment he says the inputs out loud, the conclusion is his, not yours, and it stops being a pitch.

**The multiplier version:** "You're paying $800 a month to make the phone ring. You're at your best guess 50% answer rate. So half of that $800 is lighting on fire. I'm not asking you to add a line item. I'm asking you to stop paying double for every lead you actually talk to."

**The front desk version:** "A part-time front desk person who covers evenings runs you $1,600 to $2,400 a month, plus training, plus she quits in March. This covers the phone at 9pm on a Sunday and never calls in sick."

**The peak-hour version, which is the strongest one in your category:** "When does your phone ring most? 4pm to 8pm. Where are you at 4pm to 8pm? On the mat with twenty kids. Your busiest call hour and your least available hour are the same hour. That's not a staffing problem you can fix by trying harder."

## Objection responses, with the priority flip built in

**"I can't afford it right now."**
Don't argue. Invert. "That's exactly why we should look at this. If cash is tight, the last thing you can afford is paying for leads you never speak to. Can you pull up your call log for last month? Let's count the unanswered ones and I'll shut up about it if the number is small." You've moved from asking for money to offering to find money. And you've made a falsifiable claim, which is what separates you from every other vendor cold-calling him.

**"It's summer, we're slow."**
"Slow is when this matters most. In August you'll get your back-to-school rush and you'll be too busy to answer anything. If we set it up now, it's dialed in and trained on your schedule before the wave hits, instead of you buying it in September in a panic and running it untested during your biggest enrollment month of the year."

**"My wife handles the phones."**
"How many hours a week is that? And what would she rather be doing?" Then: "This doesn't replace her. It catches the calls she physically can't get to because she's teaching the 5:30 Little Dragons class or making dinner. The 7pm call from the mom whose kid just got pushed at school, that's the highest-intent lead you'll get all week, and it's currently going to voicemail."

**"I don't want a robot talking to parents."**
Legitimate concern, don't flip it, address it. "Neither do I. Which is why the alternative I'm competing with is your voicemail, and voicemail is a worse robot. Listen to three recordings from a school like yours and tell me if you'd be embarrassed by them." Then actually play them. This objection is solved by proof, not by framing.

**"We only have 60 students, we're too small."**
"Sixty students at $170 is about $10,000 a month. Two extra enrollments is $340 a month in recurring revenue against a fraction of that in cost. Small schools have *less* front desk coverage than big ones, so the leak is proportionally worse."

**"I'll think about it."**
"Sure. Before you do, one thing: can I run a missed-call report on your line for the next 14 days? Costs you nothing. If the number comes back low, I'll tell you to skip this and I'll leave you alone."

## The single highest-leverage implementation

Build a **missed-call audit** as your lead magnet and your opener. Pull or have them pull their call data, count unanswered and after-hours calls, and convert it to dollars using their own LTV. Free, takes fifteen minutes, and it does the one thing that makes this whole philosophy work honestly: it turns an abstract "you should want this more" into a specific, verifiable number they didn't know about.

That's the legitimate version of "promise them more food later." Tate's phrasing is *make up whatever benefit gets you past the objection*. The version that builds a business that still exists in three years is *find the place where it's genuinely true, and prove it with their own data*. In your category it's almost always true, which is lucky for you. You don't have to lie to run this play.

## Where to actually walk away

Disqualify on: fewer than 25 students, no ad spend at all and no intention to start, behind on rent, or a school where the owner can't tell you his tuition rate off the top of his head. Those aren't mis-ranking you. They have a business model problem your product can't fix, and they'll churn and complain.

Everyone else is ranking you below their ad spend, and your job is to show them the ranking is backwards.

The core lesson in that transcript is **not literally “nobody is broke.”** That's an oversimplification. The useful business principle underneath it is:

> People spend money according to their perceived priorities, expected outcomes, and emotional drivers.

For your AI phone agent business, that distinction matters because martial arts school owners are not buying an AI receptionist. They're buying outcomes.

If you internalize that, your entire sales process changes.

# Principle 1: Stop Selling The Thing. Sell The Consequence.

Most AI founders pitch:

> "We have an AI voice agent that answers calls 24/7."

Nobody cares.

The school owner wakes up thinking about:

* missed leads
* declining enrollment
* instructors wasting time on the phone
* parents not calling back
* cash flow
* retention

The AI agent is just a vehicle.

### Weak pitch

"We use AI to answer incoming calls."

### Better pitch

"Every missed call is a prospective student who often calls the next school on Google. Our AI agent answers immediately, books trials, and follows up automatically."

### Better examples

Instead of:

* AI receptionist

Sell:

* More trial bookings

Instead of:

* Automated follow-up

Sell:

* Fewer no-shows

Instead of:

* Voice AI

Sell:

* More monthly memberships

Instead of:

* Call handling

Sell:

* More students without hiring staff

The transcript repeatedly says:

> Make your offer more important than the alternatives.

For a martial arts owner, your offer is competing against:

* payroll
* rent
* advertising
* new mats
* equipment
* personal income

Your job is to connect your service directly to those priorities.

# Principle 2: "I Can't Afford It" Usually Means "I Don't See The Return"

When a school owner says:

> "I can't afford $500/month."

They may genuinely be cash-constrained.

But often they really mean:

> "I don't believe spending $500 creates more than $500 in value."

Your response shouldn't be:

> "Yes you can."

Your response should be:

> "Let's look at the numbers."

### Example 1

Ask:

"How many calls do you miss per week?"

Suppose:

* 15 missed calls
* 10% become students if handled correctly
* student value = $2,000/year

Now:

15 calls × 10% × $2,000

= $3,000 potential value

Now the discussion isn't about cost.

It's about lost revenue.

---

### Example 2

Owner says:

"We already have voicemail."

You say:

"When parents call about classes for their kids, do they usually leave a voicemail or do they call the next school?"

Now you're reframing the problem.

---

### Example 3

Owner says:

"We'll hire a receptionist."

You say:

"A receptionist might cost $3,000-$5,000/month with taxes and management. Our system handles the same inbound volume for a fraction of that."

Again, you're comparing against an existing spending category.

# Principle 6: Use "Cost of Inaction"

This is the strongest application of the transcript to your business.

Don't compare:

* AI agent vs monthly fee

Compare:

* AI agent vs doing nothing

### Example

"Let's say you're missing only 5 prospective students per month."

If each student is worth:

$1,500 annually

Then:

5 × $1,500

= $7,500

Now the question becomes:

> Is losing $7,500 cheaper than spending $500?

That's the same "buy the pipe to save money" logic from the transcript, but applied professionally.
