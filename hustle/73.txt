## The idea in one sentence

Attribution data tells you **where to spend money**. Motivation data tells you **what to say when you get there**. Everyone has the first because it collects itself. Almost nobody has the second because a human has to go ask.

Tate overstates it as "nobody does this" — enterprise sales teams run win/loss interviews as a whole discipline. But at your level, in your market, he's right: I'd bet no other AI-phone vendor selling into martial arts schools has fifty verbatim answers to "what was actually happening the week you went looking for this?" That's an edge you can take in about three weeks.

One correction before you start, or you'll collect garbage.

---

## Fix 1: People don't know why they bought

Ask a gym owner "why did you buy?" and you get a rationalization: "It seemed like a good fit for where we're at." Useless. It's the answer he thinks sounds smart.

You don't want his reason. You want his **trigger** — the specific thing that happened that made him go from tolerating the problem to searching for a solution. Nobody wakes up wanting an AI phone agent. Something happened.

So don't ask why. Ask around it:

- "What was going on at the school the week you decided to go look for something like this?"
- "What was the last straw?"
- "What were you doing about the phones before, and what broke?"
- "Who else did you look at, and what made you not pick them?"
- "If we shut off tomorrow, what goes wrong first?"

Then, whatever they say, push **once**: "What made that matter?" Once, not three times. Three times and you're a therapist.

Example of the difference:

> **Q: Why did you buy?** → "We needed better lead follow-up."
> **Q: What was the last straw?** → "I pulled the call log on a Sunday and there were nineteen missed calls from the week. I recognized one of the numbers — a mom who'd toured us in January. She'd signed at the Krav place."

The first sentence gets you nothing. The second sentence *is your ad*.

## Fix 2: Ask the people who didn't buy, too

Tate only tells you to ask buyers. Buyers tell you what worked. Non-buyers tell you what's blocking everyone else — and there are far more of them. Two extra questions:

- To a demo no-show or a stalled deal: "Totally fine either way — what made this not the right move?" (Common answers you'll hear: *"I don't want a robot talking to parents." "My students would know." "I already pay for a call service."*)
- To a churned account: "What was the thing that made you pull it?"

The objections you hear repeatedly become the copy you lead with, not the copy you hide from.

## Your actual collection scripts

**Day 3 after signup, plain-text email from your personal address:**

> Subject: quick one, nothing to fix
>
> Hey [Name] — nothing's wrong, and this isn't a survey.
>
> I'm trying to understand what actually makes a school owner go looking for something like this, because the reasons are always different from what I expect.
>
> What was going on at [School Name] the week you decided to look? Even one sentence helps.

**On the onboarding call, before you touch the setup:** "Before we configure anything — what do you need this thing to do that nobody's doing right now?" The answer tells you the job. It's rarely "answer the phone."

**Opening a demo:** "What made you book this call *this* week rather than three months ago?" Best question in your arsenal. It surfaces the trigger while it's still fresh.

**Lost deal, 48 hours later:** "No pitch — what made you go a different direction?"

## The likely WHY clusters in your market, and the copy each one produces

These are hypotheses. Replace them with real verbatims as they come in. But here's the shape of what you'll probably find, and why it matters that you write from the quote and not from the concept:

**"I'm on the mat 4 to 9. That's exactly when parents call."**
→ "You teach at 5pm. Parents call at 5pm."
→ "How many calls did you miss last night while you were running class?"
→ "Your phone rings hardest during the two hours you can't answer it."

**"My wife was answering the school phone while putting our kids to bed."**
→ "Your wife is not your front desk."
→ "Get your spouse out of the front desk chair."
This is a *personal* why, not a business why. It'll outperform an ROI headline almost every time.

**"I hired a front desk person for $2,600 a month. She quit in five months."**
→ "You already tried hiring someone. How'd that go?"
→ "$2,600 a month, six weeks of training, and she quit in May."

**"I'm a black belt, not a salesman. Someone asks about price and I freeze."**
→ "You're a black belt, not a closer."
→ "You shouldn't have to sell to run a school."

**"I called the Facebook lead back the next morning. She'd already signed at the school down the road."**
→ "The gym across town called her back in 90 seconds."
→ "Your leads don't go cold in a week. They go cold in an hour."

**"The place across town has 400 students and worse instructors than me."**
→ "Better instructor. Fewer students. It's a phone problem."

Same product, six completely different ads. You can't guess which cluster is biggest. You have to count.

And notice: half of those are emotional, not financial. In B2B people expect ROI copy and write it reflexively. Tate's own War Room example is *"I wanted to feel part of a community"* — not "the ROI on the network was 4x." Your buyer is a one-man business. The personal why is usually the real one.

## The Evian test, run on your own marketing

Go look at your website and your ads right now. If you find any of this, you're writing the mountain-spring version:

- "AI-powered voice agent with natural language understanding"
- "Seamless CRM integration and 24/7 availability"
- "Trained on 10,000+ hours of conversational data"
- "Sub-200ms latency, 98% intent accuracy"

Nobody is thirsty for sub-200ms latency. The "are you thirsty" version of each:

- → "Answers every call while you're teaching."
- → "Books the trial into your calendar before the call ends."
- → "Sounds like a person. Parents don't notice."
- → "Picks up on the second ring at 8:40pm on a Saturday."

Keep the spec sheet. Put it below the fold, for the guy who's already sold and is looking for permission. It's never the hook.

## The system, so this isn't a one-off

1. **Capture** at four points: demo open, day-3 email, churn call, lost deal.
2. **Store verbatims.** One spreadsheet, four columns: date, school, question asked, exact words. Do not summarize into categories at capture time. "Reduce lead leakage" is your language; "nineteen missed calls and she signed at the Krav place" is theirs. Their language is the asset. Yours is worthless.
3. **Review every 20 answers.** Tag into clusters *after* the fact. Count them.
4. **Ship one thing per cluster.** One subject line, one ad hook, one landing page headline. Then check which one gets opened, clicked, and booked.

That last step is the part Tate leaves out and it matters. Ten answers is a hypothesis, not a fact. What people say moved them and what actually moves them diverge often enough that the ad test is the real verdict. Use the WHYs to generate candidates; use the numbers to pick the winner.

Also keep your attribution data. He's wrong that it's worthless — knowing that 70% come from one Facebook group tells you where to put the budget. It just can't tell you what to write when you get there. Where and what are two different questions and you need both.

## Run it on yourself this week

Pick the last three things you bought for the business — the CRM, the laptop, whatever.

Don't write the reason. Write the trigger. *"I bought the CRM the day I realized I'd forgotten to follow up with two schools that had asked for pricing."* Then ask what a company would have had to put on a landing page to catch you in exactly that moment. It wasn't "unified pipeline management."

Do that three times and the framework stops being something you read and starts being something you see.

**This week:** send the day-3 email to your last ten customers, and add the "why this week and not three months ago" question to your next demo. That's the whole implementation. Everything above is just what to do with the answers.

The core idea in that passage is not "ask customers for feedback."

It's:

> **People do not buy products. They buy progress toward a desired outcome.**
>
> The "why" is the outcome they are trying to achieve, the problem they are trying to escape, or the identity they want to become.

Most businesses collect:

* What they bought
* When they bought
* Where they came from
* Which ad converted them
* Which sales rep closed them

But they never systematically discover:

**"What was happening in your life/business that made you decide to buy right now?"**

For an AI phone agent business serving martial arts schools, this distinction is enormous.

# First: Understand the Different Layers of "Why"

Most business owners stop too early.

### Surface Why

"We needed someone to answer calls."

True, but weak.

### Operational Why

"We were missing leads when nobody answered."

Better.

### Emotional Why

"I was spending thousands on marketing and watching leads disappear."

Now we're getting somewhere.

### Identity Why

"I want to run a professional academy, not a chaotic small business."

This is marketing gold.

# How To Actually Collect WHYs

Don't ask:

> "How satisfied are you?"

Useless.

Don't ask:

> "Rate your experience from 1-10."

Mostly useless.

Ask:

### Question 1

> What was happening in your academy that made you start looking for a solution?

Examples:

* Too many missed calls
* Staff burnout
* Poor follow-up
* Slow growth
* Lost leads

---

### Question 2

> What nearly made you not buy?

Examples:

* Didn't trust AI
* Thought it would sound robotic
* Concerned about setup

Now you know objections.

---

### Question 3

> What convinced you to move forward?

Examples:

* Hearing recordings
* Seeing case studies
* Demo call
* Referral

Now you know what actually closes deals.

---

### Question 4

> If our AI agent disappeared tomorrow, what would you miss most?

This often reveals the deepest value.

Examples:

* Peace of mind
* Time freedom
* Lead capture
* Staff relief

# Ask WHY After Success

The best time is often after they get results.

Ask:

> Looking back, what was the real reason you decided to implement the AI agent?

Or:

> What problem were you most worried about before you started?

Or:

> What outcome were you hoping this would create for your academy?

People answer more accurately after they've experienced the value.

# Apply WHY To Yourself

The passage makes another important point:

Ask yourself why people buy from *you* specifically.

Not your AI.

You.

For example:

### Why do schools choose your company?

Maybe it's:

* You understand martial arts schools specifically.
* You speak academy-owner language.
* You know enrollment processes.
* You answer quickly.
* You customize heavily.

If customers repeatedly say:

> "You actually understand martial arts schools."

Then stop marketing yourself as:

> AI automation company.

And start marketing yourself as:

> The enrollment and communications partner built specifically for martial arts academies.

Those are completely different positions.

# Practical Weekly Process

Every week:

1. Interview 2 customers.
2. Ask 4-5 WHY questions.
3. Save exact quotes.
4. Categorize responses.
5. Count recurring themes.
6. Rewrite ads using those themes.
7. Rewrite sales calls using those themes.
8. Rewrite website headlines using those themes.

Over time you'll discover that martial arts schools are not really buying:

> AI phone agents.

They're buying things like:

* More students
* Fewer missed opportunities
* Less stress
* More professionalism
* Freedom from staffing headaches
* Confidence that every lead gets answered

Those are the WHYs.

The AI agent is simply the vehicle they use to get them. The passage's central lesson is that the vehicle is rarely the strongest thing to market. The destination usually is.
