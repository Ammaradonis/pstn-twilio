## The mechanic underneath it

Strip the swearing out and the transcript makes one sharp distinction: fake scarcity gets disbelieved, buyer-count proof gets believed. "Offer closes Friday" is a claim about *your* behavior, which you control, so the prospect reads it as manipulation. "Four schools signed up this month" is a claim about *the world*, which you don't control, so they treat it as information. Same emotion, completely different credibility.

It also does two jobs at once, which he names at the end: it proves you're a real operating business, and it lets the prospect outsource the decision. A martial arts school owner is not a trained buyer of software. He has no framework to evaluate you. So he borrows one: *other guys like me did this, so it's probably fine.*

## 1. Replace the deadline with the buyer count

His line: *"no one cares because we know that that's a lie."*

Kill anything that sounds like "price goes up Friday." Replace with volume:

- "I've got installs stacked this week, so if you want to get on, next Tuesday is realistically my first slot."
- "We turned on two schools last week, a BJJ academy and a three-location Taekwondo school."
- "Right now I'm running six schools' phones in Lower Austria."
- "Honestly the reason I'm answering you at 9pm is I've been doing setups all day."

Notice none of those are threats. They're weather reports about your business. The prospect does the panicking himself.

## 2. Specificity beats size

In a niche this small, "50 clients" is less powerful than "a school exactly like yours." Tate got away with raw numbers because he sold to a mass market. You sell to a few hundred schools in a country. Match the profile instead:

- "A gym about your size, 120 members, mostly kids' classes, turned it on six weeks ago."
- "The guy I set up last month had your exact problem, he teaches the 4-to-7 block himself, which is precisely when parents call."
- "One of my schools runs three locations and needed calls routed by postcode, so that's handled."
- "I've got a Kyokushin school and two BJJ gyms on it, and the BJJ guys get way more adult inquiries at weird hours."

You don't need names. "A school about your size, an hour from here" carries the full weight and protects your clients.

## 3. Your version of the Christmas play: the school across town

This is the strongest lever you have, and unlike his, it's structurally true. Martial arts schools fight over a 5-to-10km catchment. Same parents, same school districts, same Google Maps results. A missed call doesn't become a voicemail, it becomes an enrollment at the next dojo.

- "Are you the only school in town? Then when a mother calls at 5:40 and nobody picks up, she doesn't leave a message. She taps the next result."
- "I'll be straight with you, I'm talking to a couple of schools in your region. I'm not going to tell you which. But whoever's live first is the one catching the other one's missed calls."
- "Your competitor doesn't need to be better than you. He just needs to answer the phone while you're on the mat."
- "Every call you miss in September is worth about two thousand euros in lifetime membership. You're not losing a call, you're handing one over."

That last framing, missed call as *donated* enrollment, is the FOMO. He's not missing out on your product. He's missing out to a named human being he sees at tournaments.

## 4. The seasonal play, the version that's actually true

His Christmas-in-March bit is the one part of the transcript that's straight fabrication, and you don't need to fabricate it, because martial arts has brutally real seasons: January resolutions, back-to-school August/September, summer camp signups in spring, post-belt-test trial waves. There is always a genuine wave coming, and setup always has to happen *before* it.

- In March: "Schools sign up now because summer camp inquiries start in April and they want it live and tuned before that."
- In June: "June's the right month precisely because your phone is dead. We test it on low stakes, and by September when you're getting forty calls a week it's already dialed in."
- In October: "January is the biggest enrollment month of your year. Setting this up in January is late. You want it running two weeks before the resolution calls start."
- In August: "You're about a fortnight from back-to-school. This is the exact window."

Same script every month of the year, same effect as his, no lying required. The trick is that the reason is real, so when he pushes back you have an actual answer instead of a bluff.

## 5. Weave it in, never announce it

He's right about the delivery: *"not in a braggy way, just in a 'well I'm doing business with this dha dha dha.'"* The proof should arrive inside a subordinate clause while you're answering something else.

- Q: "Does it handle people asking about prices?" → "Yeah, one of my schools has three different kids' tiers and it quotes all of them."
- Q: "What if it gets something wrong?" → "Same thing the TKD guy asked me. I'll show you what we did for him."
- Q: "How long does setup take?" → "About two hours. I did one on Thursday, we were live by lunch."

The phrase **"one of my schools"** is the workhorse. It implies a plural, stable portfolio without ever stating a number you'd have to defend.

## 6. Make the proof ambient

His paid social-proof widget. Your equivalents, in rough order of power:

- Post a redacted recording of the agent handling a real parent call, weekly. This is proof and demo in one object, and nobody else in your niche is posting them.
- A monthly count in the places school owners actually live: Facebook owner groups, MAIA/supershow circles, Instagram. "School number fourteen went live today."
- Before/after numbers from a single client: "Thirty-one missed calls recovered in month one, nine booked trials, four signed."
- A one-page client list with logos, permission obtained, attached to every proposal.
- Get seen with known school owners at seminars and business events. Offline proof still outruns online proof in this industry.

## 7. The disruption narrative, martial-arts sized

The second half of the transcript is inevitability selling: it's coming, you already know it's coming, the window is closing, pick a side. That structure works. The apocalyptic packaging will get you laughed out of a dojo, because school owners are community-minded, family-first, often traditional people. Keep the frame, drop the bloodline stuff.

- "Within three years every school in your town will have something answering the phone. Dental practices already do. Plumbers already do. Martial arts is behind, which is exactly why it's still worth something to you."
- "You already believe this, you've used ChatGPT. The only open question is whether it answers your phone first or his."
- "The advantage isn't the software. Everyone gets the software eventually. The advantage is the eighteen months you have it before they do."
- And the direct hit on his own philosophy: "You've got a hard cap on hours. You can't teach and answer the phone at the same time, ever. This is the only part of your business where you get to stop trading your time."

## 8. If you don't have the proof yet

He skipped this because he had real numbers. Most people running this play don't, and that's where they start inventing. Your honest moves:

- Borrow the adjacent market: "AI receptionists are standard in dental now. I'm bringing it into martial arts."
- Cap a founding cohort and actually mean it: "I'm taking five schools in Austria at founding pricing because I want your before/after numbers for case studies. Then I close it." That's real scarcity, and it's verifiable.
- Convert your own product into proof: after 30 days, pull the call log, count recovered calls and booked trials, ask for two sentences. Every install becomes ammunition for the next four.

## 9. Where his version will cost you specifically

Your market is a few hundred people who all know each other. They share Facebook groups, they meet at tournaments and supershows, they cross-refer students. If you say "fifteen schools signed up in the last two weeks," someone will ask which ones, and one message in a group chat unmakes you permanently. Additionally, in Austria the UWG and the EU unfair practices rules treat invented urgency and fake purchase counters as a specific category of prohibited practice, and a competitor complaint is cheap to file. You have the rare advantage that your product generates its own verifiable proof, so there's no upside in making any of it up.

## How to actually internalize it

Build a **proof inventory** and treat it as a real asset: one line per install covering school type, size, town size, the specific problem, and the number that moved. Review it for five minutes before every call, and pick three that match the prospect's profile.

Then drill the reflex: take your twenty most common questions and rewrite every answer so a school is living inside it. Right now your answers are probably about the product. They should be about other buyers of the product.

Then a weekly ritual: one public proof post, one call log pulled, one client asked for a number. The inventory compounds, and within six months you're running his exact play with none of the exposure, because every word of it is checkable.

What you're looking at in that transcript is a combination of several persuasion principles:

1. **Social Proof** ("other people are already doing this")
2. **FOMO** ("other people are moving before you")
3. **Future Pacing** ("imagine what happens next")
4. **Opportunity Cost** ("what happens if you wait?")
5. **Category Shift** ("this isn't a purchase, it's adaptation to a major change")
6. **Status Dynamics** ("leaders act early, followers react late")

The important thing is that the strongest version is based on truth. If you exaggerate customer counts, results, or urgency, you create short-term sales and long-term trust problems.

For your business—AI phone agents for martial arts schools—here's how to internalize and implement each principle.

# 1. Social Proof: "Other schools already figured this out"

The core idea from the transcript:

> "Lots of people know something you don't know yet."

Not:

> "Buy now before we close."

Instead:

> "Other schools are already adapting."

### Example A

"One thing we've noticed is that the schools growing fastest right now aren't necessarily the schools with the best instructors. They're the schools that answer every lead immediately."

"Several school owners have realized that most missed calls are actually missed enrollments."

### Example B

"We've been talking with a surprising number of martial arts school owners who are implementing AI front-desk systems this year."

"Many owners are realizing they don't need more leads. They need to stop losing the leads they already have."

### Example C

"Five years ago, schools competed on location. Today they're competing on speed of response."

"The schools embracing AI are creating an advantage that slower schools will eventually have to catch up to."

Notice that none of these require fake numbers.

# 2. FOMO Through Market Movement

Tate's actual mechanism wasn't:

> "We're closing enrollment."

It was:

> "The train is moving."

For martial arts schools:

### Example A

"Parents expect instant responses now."

"If your school replies tomorrow and another school replies in 30 seconds, who do you think gets the trial class booked?"

### Example B

"The interesting question isn't whether AI will enter martial arts businesses."

"It's which schools adopt it first."

### Example C

"Most school owners are still thinking about generating more leads."

"The more advanced operators are focusing on converting the leads they already have."

This creates:

> "Am I behind?"

Which is stronger than:

> "Buy before Friday."
