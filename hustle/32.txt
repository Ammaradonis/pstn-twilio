Here's the thing worth extracting from that transcript: underneath the bravado there are about five actual mechanics. Everything else is style. I'll separate the mechanics from the style, give you drills to internalize each, and then script them for calls with martial arts school owners.

## The reframe: you only have one room

His opening claim — everything is a guy walking in and talking — is useful because it collapses your practice surface. You think you have five different skills. You have one, performed in different rooms:

- The cold call to a dojo at 2pm
- The Zoom demo with the owner who's still in his gi
- The booth conversation at a martial arts business expo
- The Facebook group video where you explain what the agent does
- The renewal call when month three rolls around and he's wondering if it's working

Same skill. Which means every rep counts toward all of them. Internalize this by keeping one running "talk log" — after any of those five, one line: what landed, what died. Not five separate skill trees. One.

## 1. Energy — but match the room, don't just crank it

He's right that low energy never helps. He's wrong that maximum energy always helps, and with your specific buyer that difference matters. Martial arts school owners are disciplined, often ex-military or ex-LEO, and they've been pitched by every dojo-software hustler in the industry. Screaming at them reads as the same guy who sold them a lead-gen package that didn't work.

What actually transfers is **intensity**, not volume. Conviction, pace, no trailing off at the end of sentences.

Low energy (you, probably, on call #40 of the day):
> "So yeah, um, basically what it does is it, like, answers the phone when you can't... and it can kind of book people in, depending on your setup..."

High energy, hype version (wrong room):
> "Bro, this thing is a MACHINE, it's gonna 10X your intros, I'm telling you right now —"

High intensity, right room:
> "Here's what it does. Your phone rings at 6:40 on a Tuesday. You're on the mat with fourteen kids. Nobody picks up. That caller is gone — they call the next school on the list. My agent picks up on ring two, talks like a human, books the intro, texts them the confirmation. You walk off the mat and there's a name in your calendar."

Same information. The third one has a spine. Drill: record yourself giving your 60-second explainer three times — once flat, once as a hype man, once as a black belt explaining a technique to a serious student. Keep the third. That's your register.

## 2. Kill the "uh" with a bridge

This is the best concrete trick in the whole transcript, and it works exactly as he says. The filler exists because your mouth starts before your brain finishes. The fix is to give your mouth something pre-loaded to say while your brain catches up. He repeats the question. That's one bridge. Build yourself four or five.

**Bridge type A — repeat and commit:**
> Owner: "What happens if the AI can't answer something?"
> You: "Great question, and I'm going to tell you exactly what happens when it can't answer something. Three things."

**Bridge type B — name the real question underneath:**
> Owner: "How much is it?"
> You: "What you're really asking is whether this pays for itself. Let me answer that first, then I'll give you the number."

**Bridge type C — the frame-setter:**
> Owner: "We already have a front desk person three days a week."
> You: "Then the only thing that matters here is the other four days. Let's look at those."

**Bridge type D — the count:**
> Owner: "Does it integrate with Kicksite?" (or Zen Planner, PushPress, ClubReady)
> You: "Two parts to that. First the calendar, second the student records. Calendar —"

Announcing a number buys you a full second and makes you sound organized. You'll be amazed how often "three things" turns out to be three things.

Drill for internalizing: write your twelve most common objections and questions on cards — pricing, "will it sound robotic," "what if it books someone wrong," "I don't want to lose the personal touch," "my students know my voice," "what about after-hours," "does it handle Spanish," "contract length," "who trains it on my class schedule." Pull one at random, hit record, and the rule is you must be speaking within half a second and you may not say uh. Do ten a day. Two weeks and it's automatic.

Second drill, the one he doesn't mention: slow down. Fillers are a speed symptom. Talk at 80% of your natural rate for a week and the ums vanish on their own, then let speed come back.

## 3. "There's no proposal" — the part everyone skips

He says people have the deck, the product, the price, and still lose because there's no proposal. That's the single most valuable line in the transcript and he buries it. A proposal means you told them what to do. Most calls end in a fog of "so let me know what you think."

Weak close:
> "So that's pretty much it — any questions? Feel free to think it over."

Proposal close, three versions:
> "Here's what I want to do. Give me your main number for 14 days. I'll run the agent on missed calls only — your team keeps answering everything they can get to. On day 15 I show you the list of calls that would have gone to voicemail and what happened instead. If the list is unimpressive, we shake hands and I'm gone."

> "Two options. Option one, we go live Monday on your main line. Option two, we point it at your Google Business number only, so it's ring-fenced to new leads and touches nothing else. I'd do option two with a school your size. Which is it?"

> "You've got the belt test Saturday, so I'm not going to ask you to make a decision today. What I am going to ask is fifteen minutes Monday at 11 with your calendar open, because setup takes eleven of them. Does Monday at 11 work?"

Every talk ends with a proposal. Even the Facebook video ends with one.

## 4. Sell the result, not the ebook

He tosses this off mid-sentence and it's the most directly applicable line to your business, because "AI phone agent" is a thing and nobody wants a thing.

What you're actually selling, ranked by how much a school owner cares:

- Booked intro appointments while he's teaching
- The missed call that was a mom with two kids at $320/month
- Not paying $2,400/month for a front-desk person
- Not having his wife answer the phone at 8pm anymore

Translate it every time:

> Feature: "24/7 availability." → Result: "The kid's dad who Googles jiu-jitsu at 9:40pm after the kids are in bed — he books right then instead of forgetting by morning."

> Feature: "Custom knowledge base." → Result: "It knows your trial offer, your belt curriculum, and that you don't take four-year-olds in the adult program. It won't book you a bad fit."

> Feature: "SMS follow-up." → Result: "Every no-show gets a text the next morning. You do nothing."

> Feature: "Call transcripts and analytics." → Result: "End of month, you can see exactly how many people wanted to give you money and how many actually got booked."

The sharpest version is arithmetic done out loud. Average student value at a martial arts school runs high — a kid on a family program at $200/month who stays 18 months is roughly $3,600. So: "If this catches one extra intro a month and you close half of them, tell me what that's worth against what I'm charging." Let him do the multiplication himself; he'll believe his own number.

## 5. Find your own angle

He's honest that you can't copy him. So reverse-engineer instead. Next three engaging people you encounter — a podcaster, a guy at a seminar, whoever — name the specific mechanic: is it the pauses, the laugh, the concrete detail, the willingness to say something slightly disagreeable, the fact that he asks a question and then actually shuts up?

For your market, a few angles that fit better than hype:
- **Operator credibility** — if you've trained, say so early and casually. "I've taken the 6am class, I know what the phone does during it."
- **Specificity** — names of software, names of belt ranks, the fact that Tuesday and Thursday 6pm is when the phone rings most. Detail is charisma for skeptical buyers.
- **Calm certainty** — the guy who isn't in a hurry is the guy who doesn't need the deal.
- **Willingness to disqualify** — "If you're doing under 30 inbound calls a month, don't buy this." Nothing builds trust faster.

## The weekly loop

1. Ten objection-card reps a day, no fillers, must start within half a second.
2. Record every real call you're allowed to. Listen to the first 30 seconds and the last 30 seconds only — that's where deals are won and lost.
3. One line in the talk log per conversation.
4. Once a week, deliver your 60-second explainer to a real human who isn't a prospect and watch their face for the moment they drift.

One honest caveat on his framing: he claims 99% of people lose the deal on an "um," and that's theater. Buyers forgive a filler; they don't forgive vagueness, no proposal, and not knowing their business. Train the fillers out anyway — it's cheap and it works — but if you only have energy to fix one thing this month, fix the proposal.

The core idea in that transcript is not really "talk louder" or "be like this speaker." The deeper lesson is:

> Your ability to influence outcomes is largely determined by your ability to communicate ideas with clarity, confidence, and engagement.

For an AI phone agent business serving martial arts schools, this matters because you're not selling software. You're selling outcomes:

* More trial bookings
* Fewer missed leads
* Better follow-up
* More memberships
* More revenue
* Less admin work

The transcript repeatedly emphasizes that people often have a great product but fail because they communicate it poorly.

Let's turn that into practical systems.

# Principle 1: Stop Selling the Product. Sell the Result.

The speaker says:

> "You don't sell the ebook. You sell the result of the ebook."

For you:

### Weak pitch

"We build AI phone agents for martial arts schools."

That's a feature.

### Better pitch

"We help martial arts schools answer every lead call, even when instructors are teaching classes."

That's a result.

### Better

"Most martial arts schools lose potential students because nobody answers the phone during classes. Our AI agent answers instantly and books trials automatically."

Now the owner sees money.

### Better

"If your school misses just three trial students per month, that can easily be thousands in annual membership revenue. Our AI agent helps capture those opportunities."

Now you're selling economics.

Train yourself to always speak at the outcome level.

# Principle 4: Eliminate "Um" By Replacing It

One of the most useful points in the transcript.

Most people do this:

Customer:

> "How exactly does the AI work?"

Response:

> "Uh... well... um..."

This immediately lowers perceived expertise.

---

Instead use a bridge phrase.

Customer:

> "How exactly does the AI work?"

You:

> "Great question. Here's exactly how it works."

Now your brain has time.

---

More examples:

### Question

"How is this different from a receptionist?"

Response:

"That's the key difference. Let me explain."

---

### Question

"What happens if somebody asks something unusual?"

Response:

"Good question. Here's how we handle that."

---

### Question

"Will this work for my school?"

Response:

"Let's talk about that specifically."

---

These phrases buy thinking time while sounding confident.

# Principle 5: Slow Down Before Speeding Up

The speaker mentions that fluent speakers don't start fast.

Fast = confident

Not true.

Fast + clear = confident.

Fast + sloppy = nervous.

### Right

# Principle 8: Use Question-Led Selling

Most bad sales calls look like this:

Pitch.
Pitch.
Pitch.
Pitch.

Instead:

Question.
Question.
Question.

Then pitch.

---

For martial arts schools:

"Who answers the phone right now?"

"How often are instructors teaching when calls come in?"

"What happens after hours?"

"Do you know how many calls go to voicemail each week?"

"How many trial lessons do you book per month?"

Now the owner is discovering the problem themselves.

People believe conclusions they arrive at more than conclusions they're told.
