The core of this lesson holds up, and it fits your niche unusually well. People judge your discipline from your body and behavior before they weigh your words. Your buyers are martial arts school owners, who spend all day judging people by how they carry themselves, whether they show up, and whether they quit when it gets hard. Below I keep what's true, apply it to selling AI phone agents, and flag the parts I'd drop.

## 1. Your buyers read physical presence better than almost anyone

Tate says humans respect "physical specimens." For most salespeople that's a small edge. For you it's the first filter, because a school owner has spent years sizing up new students at the door.

- **In-person demos:** The owner notices your posture, your handshake, and whether you look like you train before you open your laptop. By the time you start the pitch, they've already half-decided whether you're serious.
- **Zoom demos:** Sit upright, put the camera at eye level, and light your face. On a screen, framing is your presence, and the owner is often sitting behind a front desk in a rashguard, comparing you to their own instructors.
- **Seminars, tournaments, association meetups:** You're standing next to people who fight for a living. Looking like you belong gets you conversations that a pitch deck never would.

## 2. Train in their world, not just in the weight room

Tate's authority came from being a fighter, not just from lifting. The strongest version of this for you is time on the mats.

- **Take a drop-in class at a prospect's school before you pitch.** You earn respect, and you see the real problem: the owner is teaching the 6pm class while the front-desk phone rings with nobody there to answer it.
- **Have your own rank journey.** Saying "I'm a blue belt at such-and-such academy" builds rapport faster than any case study.
- **Speak their language.** Talk about trial classes, belt testing, kids' program retention, and the dropout after the first month, not "lead conversion funnels."

Tate skips a catch here: mat culture rewards humility. An "alpha" pitch to a third-degree black belt goes badly. The respected person in a gym bows in, taps without ego, and thanks his partners. Bring that energy to sales and it reads as trustworthy. Posturing reads as someone who'll be a problem later.

## 3. What's really being signaled is discipline, so show it everywhere

Tate says a fit body tells people you're "not afraid of hard work" and "disciplined," without a word spoken. Your body is one signal. Your operations are the one the owner feels directly.

- **Answer your own phone instantly.** You sell "never miss a call." If a prospect calls you and hits voicemail, you're Tate's out-of-shape guy saying "trust me, it works." Put your own AI agent on your line so every inbound call doubles as a demo.
- **Be early and follow through.** Join demos early, send the recap the same day, and follow up exactly when you said you would. Owners notice this, because it's what they demand from students.
- **Onboard fast.** Build the agent around their class schedule, pricing, and trial offer, and have it live in days, not weeks. This is Tate's speed tenet applied where the client actually feels it.

## 4. Your word carries weight, so don't spend it on guarantees you can't control

This is the part of the script I'd change. "Spend 19 grand and you get double back, I guarantee it" works in the room. But you can't control whether a school's instructors convert trial classes, how they price memberships, or whether their ads are any good. When an outcome guarantee fails, the respect you built makes it feel like a bigger betrayal. And the martial arts world is small: owners talk at seminars, in affiliation networks, and in Facebook groups. Outcome guarantees can also create refund and consumer-protection issues depending on how you word them (I'm not a lawyer, so check this).

Keep Tate's structure of diagnosing with questions and then committing confidently, but promise what you control.

- **His Tesco questioning, adapted:**
  - You: "How many calls come in while you're teaching?"
  - Owner: "No idea, nobody's at the desk during evening classes."
  - You: "Where do your new students come from?"
  - Owner: "Mostly Facebook ads."
  - You: "So you're paying for leads that ring out while you're on the mat."
- **Commitments you control:** "Every call gets answered, day or night, and every caller gets a text with a trial booking link within a minute." Or: "I'll go through the first week of call recordings with you and tune the script myself." Or: "If after 60 days you don't think it's worth it, cancel with no contract."
- **Proof before the pitch:** Call the school during class time and see whether anyone picks up. Then open with "I called you Tuesday at 6:30 and it rang out." That lands harder than any guarantee.

## 5. Tate's 10% math on your numbers

He argues that a 10% edge separates a little money from a lot. Here it is with illustrative numbers you can swap for your real ones:

- **Baseline:** 100 demos a year, a 20% close rate, and $300 a month per school gives you 20 schools and $72,000 a year.
- **Better close rate:** If credibility lifts your close rate to 25%, that's 25 schools and $90,000 a year.
- **Lower churn:** If trust also keeps the average school for 18 months instead of 12, each school is worth $5,400 instead of $3,600.
- **Referrals:** Respected vendors get recommended in owner networks, so the gains compound beyond the math above.

## 6. "Would you have bought this course?" Apply that test to your marketing

Tate argues people bought before hearing a word because of how he looked. Ask the same question about everything prospects see from you.

- **Demo video:** A demo filmed in a real dojo lobby with you on camera beats a screen recording with a voiceover.
- **Borrowed presence:** A respected head instructor saying the agent booked a pile of trial classes last month carries more weight than anything you say about yourself.
- **Social profiles:** Training content alongside business content tells owners you're one of them, not a tech guy who found a niche on a spreadsheet.

## What I'd leave out

The webcam business example doesn't translate, and I wouldn't reference it around clients. I'd also drop the idea that Dan could "get away with" how he treated staff because of his size. In a tight-knit niche, how you treat people is your reputation, and physical presence makes mistreatment land harder, not softer. The goal also isn't to look like a professional fighter. It's to look like someone who keeps promises to himself.
