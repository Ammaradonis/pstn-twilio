The part of this that actually works isn't the refund itself. It's the trust the refund signals. Tate gets that trust by lying: the "some things have changed" story, the ebook update that never happens, the "$100" product, the apology for a delay he invented. He calls it "DIRTY manipulation" himself. I won't help you script that part. It's also a worse fit for your business than for his, for a few reasons:

- **Your customers don't disappear.** Tate's "Goose" pays once and never matters again. A martial arts school owner pays you every month, so if they later feel played, you lose every future payment, not just one sale.
- **Your results can be checked.** Tate says the ebook buyer "never saw the original" and can't tell whether anything changed. Your product produces call logs and booked trials, so if you claim "market conditions changed" and nothing did, the owner will notice.
- **Your niche talks.** School owners know each other through associations, seminars, Facebook groups, and the same membership software. A story about "the AI phone guy who refunded me and came back 50% higher" spreads fast.
- **It carries legal risk.** Misleading reasons for price changes and invented "worth $X" values can breach unfair-competition rules, and in many countries those rules protect business buyers too. I'm not a lawyer, so check locally.

Every mechanism underneath the trick can be used honestly, though. Here's each one, tied to the text, with examples for your business.

## 1. A refund builds trust because it costs you something

The text says it works "because no one gives refunds." That's true: a refund is believable precisely because it costs you money. The honest version is to refund or pause billing only when it's actually the right call. In your niche that happens more often than you'd think:

- **Too few calls.** A school gets about 20 calls a month and the front desk answers nearly all of them, so the agent would barely change anything. Refund them and say, "Call me when you open the second location or start running ads."
- **Booking isn't connected yet.** If the agent isn't linked to their booking software, it can only take messages, not book trial classes. Pause billing until it can do the job they paid for.
- **The phone number is about to change.** If the school is moving or switching numbers next month, starting now means paying to set up twice. Hold off until the new number is live.
- **Nothing to sell on the phone.** Some owners won't state prices or a trial offer over the phone, so the agent has nothing to convert callers with. Refund, help them define an offer, then launch.

You can also make this a standing policy instead of a one-off move. For example: "If the agent doesn't book at least X trial classes in your first 60 days, I refund your setup fee." Pick a number you're confident you'll hit, and honor it without arguing.

## 2. The takeaway: "not the right time"

Tate's line "I don't think now is the optimal time" is a takeaway, and takeaways work. The honest version is a real readiness check you tell customers about before they pay:

- **Onboarding checklist.** The school confirms its class schedule, trial offer, pricing policy, common FAQs, and call forwarding. Billing starts when the checklist is done, not when they sign.
- **Enrollment seasons.** January and back-to-school are the rushes. If someone signs in late December, recommend a week of test calls before going live, so the agent isn't learning during the busiest week of the year.
- **Staged rollout.** If an owner is nervous, start with after-hours calls only for two weeks, then expand. Only claim limited onboarding slots if you actually have a cap.

## 3. Trust leads to a bigger second sale

The text says "you built so much trust... that they'll spend more later." That's right, but results build that trust far more reliably than a story does:

- **30-day report.** Send each owner calls answered, after-hours calls caught, and trial classes booked. The upsell conversation starts from that report.
- **Add-ons that fit the niche.** Examples: text follow-ups for trial no-shows, calls to reactivate old leads and lapsed members, handling birthday party and summer camp inquiries, a second location, or a second language.
- **Price against real numbers.** "The agent booked 14 trials. If you convert half at your membership price, that's several times what the upgrade costs." That's the honest version of the "$2,000 now $3,000" jump.

## 4. Bundles: "worth $100, both for $40"

Bundles work, but the "worth" number has to be real:

- **Separate prices on your site.** Base agent plus follow-up texts plus lead reactivation costs less together than separately.
- **Annual prepay.** If you normally charge a setup fee, waive it for an annual commitment.
- **Founding-client pricing.** A real discount for your first 10 schools, with a real end date you stick to.

## 5. Contact people personally

"Email them in person so they have to see it... Don't just click refund." Keep this part as it is:

- **Pausing or refunding.** Phone the owner yourself rather than letting an automated email do it.
- **Explaining the pause.** Send a 2-minute screen recording of their call data showing why you're recommending it.
- **Following up.** End every pause call with a specific date to check back in, so it doesn't feel like you've dropped them.

A pause call might sound like: *"Before I charge you, I looked at your setup. Your booking system isn't connected yet, so right now the agent can take messages but can't book trials, and that's the whole point. I've refunded you. Once it's connected, we launch, and I'll send you a 30-day report so you can see exactly what it's doing."*

## 6. The math: make each buyer worth more

Tate's point that only 1 of 100 people buys is the most useful line in the text. If that's true, your gains come from average contract value, not deception:

- **Three tiers.** For example: after-hours only, full coverage, and full coverage plus follow-up and reactivation. Most buyers pick the middle.
- **Annual plans.** Offer an annual option with a real discount.
- **Pricing by size.** Charge per location, or add a tier for large schools with multiple instructors and high call volume.

## 7. Stay organized

"Keep a spreadsheet or something SMARTER." Agreed:

- **CRM stages.** Use stages like "Paid – not ready," "Paused," "Live – 30-day review," and "Upgrade offered."
- **Reminders.** Set an automatic task for the check-back date you promised.
- **Two numbers to track.** Watch how many paused schools come back, and how many live schools upgrade after their 30-day report.

The honest version still gets you what Tate is after: more trust, a clean second pitch, and higher revenue per customer. The difference is that the trust holds up when the owner looks at their call logs six months later.

The core idea in that transcript is **not actually “send money back.”** The deeper mechanism is:

1. Create trust through an unexpected pro-customer action.
2. Demonstrate that you're willing to sacrifice short-term revenue.
3. Position yourself as an advisor rather than a seller.

In the long run, especially in B2B services like AI phone agents for martial arts schools, fake scarcity and fabricated reasons destroy referrals, retention, and reputation.

The useful lesson is:

> Use genuine acts of customer advocacy to earn trust, then ethically expand the relationship.

For your AI phone-agent business, that can be extremely powerful.

# Principle 1: Become the advisor, not the vendor

In the transcript:

> "I don't think now is the optimal time."

What actually worked wasn't the refund.

It was:

> "I am willing to lose money because I care about your result."

Martial arts school owners hear sales pitches constantly.

Most AI companies say:

* "Buy now."
* "Sign today."
* "Limited offer."

Almost nobody says:

* "You're not ready yet."

### Example A: School misses too many calls

Owner says:

> "I want your AI agent."

You discover:

* They answer 95% of calls already.
* Front desk is excellent.

Instead of selling:

> "Honestly, I don't think you're the right fit yet. You're already answering most leads. I'd rather talk again in 60 days if call volume increases."

Most competitors won't do this.

Now you're trusted.

---

### Example B: School has no lead flow

Owner wants AI.

You find:

* Website gets almost no traffic.
* Only 3 inquiries per week.

You say:

> "An AI phone agent won't move the needle right now. You need more inbound leads first."

That's the ethical version of Tate's move.

You lose a sale.

You gain authority.

# Principle 7: Make "no" part of your sales process

One hidden lesson from the transcript:

Most salespeople never say no.

The person who occasionally refuses business appears more credible.

During discovery:

Paradoxically, this often increases future conversions.
