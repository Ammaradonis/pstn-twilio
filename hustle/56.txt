## The mechanic underneath it

Strip the examples away and the principle is this: **curiosity is the gap between something you know exists and something you don't know yet.** No gap, no pull. Which means most of the work isn't withholding information — it's *installing the question first.*

Tate skips past this, but it's the part that matters for you. "I have 100 business secrets" only works because the listener already wants money. The question was pre-installed. Your prospect — a martial arts school owner — does not wake up wondering how his phone line performs. He thinks it's fine. So for you the sequence is two steps, not one:

1. Make him aware there's something about his own business he doesn't know.
2. Don't tell him.

Almost every bad curiosity attempt fails at step 1. "Discover the secret to more students" creates nothing, because there's no specific gap — just a vague gesture at one.

## One place this advice will cost you money

The bouncy ball bit — "even if it is no different, you go 'it bounces better,' client says no it doesn't, you say yeah it does, who gives a shit" — works for one-shot sales to strangers. You're selling recurring software into a niche where every owner is in the same three Facebook groups, the same association, the same regional seminars. Martial arts school owners talk constantly. A curiosity gap you can't pay off becomes "that AI phone guy overpromises" inside a month, and that reputation is unrecoverable in a market this small.

So: use every mechanic below, but make every loop you open one you can actually close. The good news is you're sitting on real mystery — the owner genuinely doesn't know what his phone is doing. You never have to invent any.

## The six moves, translated to your business

### 1. Sell the end result, never the contents list

Tate's ebook point: "my ebook contains X, Y, Z" versus "I'll tell you the secrets to making money." The contents list closes the loop. The result opens one, because the buyer has to wonder *how*.

**What you're probably doing:**
> "AI phone agent with 24/7 answering, automated trial booking, calendar sync, SMS follow-up, and CRM integration."

Every one of those closes a loop. He reads it, understands it, feels nothing.

**Versions that leave the loop open:**
- "Your phone gets answered on the third ring at 8:40pm on a Saturday, and the person on the other end books a trial. You won't be there."
- "You find out how many students your phone line has been costing you every month. Then you stop paying it."
- "Every call gets answered. The part owners don't expect is what happens to the ones who *don't* book."
- "Your front desk stops being whichever brown belt is closest to the phone."

The mechanic in each: he can picture the outcome but not the method. That gap is the click.

### 2. Word choice does enormous work

"Secrets, not lessons." The idea has the same content; the word changes whether there's a gap in it. Audit your vocabulary:

| You're saying | Say instead |
|---|---|
| Free phone audit | The leak report |
| Book a demo | Hear your own phone line |
| Features | What it does at 8:47 on a Saturday |
| Case study | What the 400-student schools do differently |
| Onboarding call | The setup nobody sees |
| Pricing | What it costs vs. what the missed calls cost |

Also: **name your mechanism.** An unnamed process has no mystery; a named one implies there's a thing to learn. "The Third Ring Rule." "The 6PM Problem." "The Mat-Side Test." When a school owner hears "most schools fail the Mat-Side Test," he has to ask what it is. He cannot help it. If you'd said "most schools don't answer the phone well," he'd nod and scroll.

### 3. Install the question, then withhold the answer

This is your highest-leverage move and it's nearly free, because the truth is mysterious enough.

**Ways to install the question:**
- "Call your own school right now, during the 5:30 kids class. Use your cell. I'll wait."
- "There's one number every school owner should know and maybe three percent do: how many people called you last month and didn't leave a message."
- "Ask whoever's at your desk tonight how many calls came in yesterday. Watch what happens."
- "You know your show-up rate for trials. Do you know your *pick-up* rate?"
- "Between 5 and 8pm you have twelve people on the mat and nobody on the phone. Those are the same hours everybody calls."

Each one drops a question into his head about *his own business* that he can't answer. He now has an itch you created and only you can scratch. That's the position Tate's describing — you just got there through a true observation instead of a made-up one.

**Then withhold:**
- "I ran that check on 40 schools in Austria. The average was worse than owners guessed by a factor of about four. I'll show you yours."
- "I know what your phone did last Tuesday between six and eight. Want me to send it over?"

### 4. The open loop with a wait attached

The cam girl example — "you'll have to wait and see" — is about refusing to itemize when asked. Refusal, held pleasantly, is more interesting than the answer.

When a prospect asks the itemizing question, don't itemize:

- **"What does it actually say to people?"** → "That's the part I like. It doesn't sound like the AI on your chiropractor's line, and most callers never work it out. I'd rather play you a real recording than describe it."
- **"How's it different from [competitor]?"** → "One specific thing, and it's the reason schools switch. Take the call and I'll show you inside two minutes."
- **"What's the price?"** → "Less than one student. The interesting question is the one I'll ask you on the call." *(Careful with this one — B2B buyers will punish you for a real price dodge. Use it as a beat, not a wall. Give the number if he asks twice.)*
- **"Does it handle kids class questions?"** → "It handles the question you're actually worried about, which isn't that one. I'll show you."

### 5. The bonus you don't name

"Buy my book and get a free gift." "What's the gift?" "Buy the book."

Your versions:
- "Every school that onboards this month also gets the Script. I don't put it on the site and I don't send it separately."
- "You'll get one thing in week two that I only give to schools that actually run the system. Owners tend to say it was worth more than the software."
- "There's a second thing included. I'll tell you what it is when you're set up."

Make it real and make it good — a genuinely useful phone script, a recorded teardown of their calls, a benchmark report against schools in their region. In B2B the unnamed bonus buys you the meeting; a disappointing one costs you the renewal.

### 6. Insider versus outsider

"You wanted to be on the inside, not the outside." School owners feel this hard — most of them are excellent martial artists and self-taught businesspeople, and they suspect other people know things they don't.

- "The schools that broke 300 students all handle the phone the same way. It's not the way you're doing it."
- "There's a reason the big academies never have the owner answering the phone, and it's not the money."
- "I work with five schools per city. Two open in Vienna right now."
- "I send a monthly breakdown to the schools on the system — pick-up rates, peak call hours, what leads actually ask. It doesn't go anywhere else."

## How to internalize it (so it becomes reflex, not homework)

**The filter question.** Before anything ships — email, ad, DM, landing page, even a voicemail — ask one thing: *what does this make him want to ask me?* If the answer is "nothing," it's dead copy. Rewrite. Tate's version of this is "don't put out any company messaging without a tinge of curiosity." That's the reflex you're building.

**The nod test.** Read your message as the school owner. Can he nod and move on? If yes, you closed every loop. Something must be left unclosed.

**The 90/10 rule.** Every message delivers 90% and holds 10%. Not 50/50 — that's clickbait and it reads as evasive to a business buyer. You want him thinking "this guy clearly knows, and there's one more thing."

**Daily drill, ten minutes.** Take five pieces of copy — yours, competitors', anything in your inbox — and rewrite each headline so one specific thing is withheld. Then rewrite the withheld thing so it's *believable and about something he already cares about*. That second constraint is where most people fail. "Secret" about something he doesn't care about generates zero pull.

**Swipe file.** Every subject line or DM that made *you* open, save it, and write one line on what gap it opened. You're training pattern recognition, not collecting templates.

**One loop per message.** Stacked mysteries read as manipulation. One gap, clean, then pay it off.

## Where to deploy it across your funnel

- **Cold email subject line:** "your 6:40pm call" / "Tuesday, 18:42" / "your phone, one thing" — specific enough to be real, incomplete enough to open.
- **First line of the email:** install the question. "Do you know what your line does during the kids class?"
- **DM:** "Ran something on your school's number. Want it?" (Nothing else. Let him ask.)
- **Landing page headline:** result + method withheld. Contents list goes *below* the fold, never above it.
- **Demo call open:** "Before I show you anything — guess how many calls you missed last month. I'll tell you the real number after."
- **Follow-up after no-reply:** "Still have that recording. Say the word." Open loops survive follow-ups better than value props do.
- **Post-purchase:** the unnamed bonus in week two. Curiosity reduces early churn, because he's waiting for something.

The whole thing compresses to one sentence you can carry around: **make him aware of a gap in his own business, prove you can see into it, don't close it until he's on a call with you.**

The core idea in that transcript is not really "mystery" by itself. It's a specific marketing principle:

> People often value discovering information more than receiving information.

The speaker is arguing that **curiosity creates attention**, and attention creates engagement, conversations, leads, and sales.

For your business—AI phone agents for martial arts schools—the mistake would be explaining the technology too early.

Most AI companies market like this:

> "We offer AI voice agents that answer calls, qualify leads, schedule appointments, integrate with CRMs, and provide 24/7 support."

Nobody cares.

A martial arts school owner doesn't wake up wanting AI voice agents.

They wake up wanting:

* More students
* Fewer missed calls
* More trial bookings
* Less admin work
* More revenue

The curiosity angle is to sell the outcome first and leave an information gap.

The transcript repeatedly says:

The owner immediately thinks:

Now they're curious.

# Principle 2: Create an Open Loop

An open loop is when you start a story without finishing it.

The human brain wants closure.

The transcript is essentially saying:

> Start loops. Don't close them immediately.

---

### Example

Bad:

> "We increased trial bookings by 27%."

Interesting, but finished.

---

Better:

> "A simple phone change helped one martial arts school increase trial bookings by 27%."

Now they're wondering:

> What change?

---

Better still:

> "We discovered something about martial arts inquiries that most schools completely overlook."

Now they're wondering:

> What is it?

One of the strongest curiosity triggers is revealing a problem people don't know they have.

Now they're thinking:

> Is that true?

> How much is it costing me?

---

> "The busiest time for new student inquiries isn't when most school owners think."

Each creates curiosity because it challenges an existing belief.

# Principle 6: Reveal Information Gradually

Most founders dump everything at once.

Curiosity marketing works because each piece of content creates another question.

Think:

### Post 1

> Most martial arts schools underestimate how many leads they're losing from missed calls.

Question created:

> How many?

---

### Post 2

> We measured it.

> The number was larger than most owners expected.

Question:

> What number?

---

### Post 3

> Here's how top-performing schools are solving it.

Question:

> How?

---

Instead of giving everything away in one post.

---

# Principle 7: Curiosity Must Lead to a Real Payoff

This is where many marketers fail.

The transcript sometimes borders on:

> "Create mystery even if there's no difference."

That's risky.

If curiosity repeatedly leads nowhere, trust collapses.

The better formula is:

**Curiosity → Discovery → Value**

Not:

**Curiosity → Nothing**

---

For example:

### Good

> "The first 15 seconds of an inquiry call matter more than most owners realize."

Then actually show why.

---

### Bad

> "The shocking secret martial arts schools don't want you to know!!!"

And then reveal something obvious.

People feel manipulated.
