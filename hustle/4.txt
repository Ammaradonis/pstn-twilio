The core of this text holds up: trust beats talent, speed wins, pooling resources compounds, and young people often understand new tech better than you do. One part needs rebuilding, because as written it would hurt your business, and the second half of the same text explains why. Below are the principles, each tied back to the original and applied to selling AI phone agents to martial arts schools.

## 1. You already have a team. You just haven't mapped it.

**From the text:** *"You already have workers for your business. You already have them. You just don't use them."*

The first step is a list, not a job posting. Write down everyone you know and match them to the jobs your business actually has: selling, building and configuring agents, making content, admin, and opening doors.

- **The call-center cousin.** Someone who's worked a call center, a restaurant host stand, or a front desk knows what messy inbound calls sound like. They can write the agent's call flows and role-play the difficult callers.
- **The friend who trains.** A sibling or friend who trains at a dojo can introduce you to their head instructor. One warm intro to a school owner beats fifty cold emails.
- **The 19-year-old who lives on TikTok.** They can turn "this school missed 14 calls last month" into a 30-second video that gym owners actually watch.
- **The relative who does bookkeeping.** A few paid hours a month on invoicing, payments, and taxes frees you to sell.
- **The friend who sells to local businesses.** Someone doing websites or ads for gyms already has owners in their contacts, which makes them a natural referral partner.

## 2. Use young people's tech fluency, but pay them properly

**From the text:** *"Young people know more than the old people anyway especially about technology"* and *"I love hiring 18 or 19 year old kids."*

That part is right. Drop the rest of that section: the free work, manipulating a 15-year-old, and making him a "director" with 10% who has to "do everything I say." There are three reasons.

- **Legal risk.** In most countries, unpaid work for a for-profit business generally counts as employment that must be paid. There are strict rules for anyone under 18, and minors usually can't be company directors or hold equity without guardians involved. I'm not a lawyer, so check your local rules.
- **Product risk.** Your product answers a real school's phone. If an unpaid, unsupervised teenager misconfigures it and tells a parent the wrong trial-class time, the school owner blames *you*, not the kid.
- **It contradicts the text's own point.** The second half is about building a team of people who'd never betray you. You don't build that loyalty by manipulating a teenager with promises of being "rich by 20."

The version that works focuses on 18–22-year-olds, which matches his own stated preference. Give them clearly defined tasks, fair pay per task, and upside on top. Here's his $300 website example rebuilt for your business (numbers are illustrative):

- **Onboarding.** You charge a school a $500 setup fee. A young helper loads the class schedule, pricing, trial-booking rules, and FAQs into the agent for a flat $100–150. You handle the sale and check the work. It's the same middleman math as the text, done fairly.
- **Test calls.** Pay per batch of 30 test calls where they play a nervous parent, a guy asking about MMA pricing, or someone trying to cancel, and log every failure.
- **Content.** Pay a flat fee per short video showing the agent booking a trial class at 10pm on a Sunday.
- **Lead lists.** Pay per verified list of schools in a region, including whether their phone goes to voicemail after hours. Use public business information only.
- **Commission.** Offer 10–20% of a new school's first months for anyone who brings one in. This is the honest version of "they have nothing to lose": someone living at home can accept commission-heavy pay that a 30-year-old with rent can't. The commission should come on top of fair pay, not replace it.

## 3. Speed: "Tomorrow, show me it"

**From the text:** *"They say 'a week.' I say 'no, it's gonna be ready tomorrow.'"*

- **Demo within 24 hours.** After a discovery call, have a demo agent answering in the school's name, with their schedule, by the next day. Letting the owner call it themselves is often what closes the deal.
- **New objections answered the next day.** When an owner asks "what if it gets a price question wrong?", turn it into a scripted answer and a short video by tomorrow.
- **Same-day tests.** When you want to try a new voice or greeting, test it the same day rather than next week.
- **One adjustment for your business.** Nothing fast goes onto a live client's phone line without a check. In the text, speed is internal; for you, mistakes are customer-facing.

## 4. Pool resources like the six brothers

**From the text:** the brothers in different professions sharing one house and one Ferrari, and the Tates splitting an Aston Martin and putting the savings *"into business."*

Two ideas are packed in here: specialize so nobody duplicates work, and share costs so more money goes into growth.

- **Split roles like the brothers.** You sell and own client relationships, a brother or friend builds and maintains agents, a cousin handles content, and someone handles finance.
- **Share equipment.** One good mic, camera, and lighting kit for demo videos serves the whole team. One shared workspace replaces two.
- **Share living costs.** Early on, one flat instead of two frees up money for months of ads, or for trips to tournaments and seminars where school owners gather.
- **Share lead generation.** Team up with the friend who does websites for gyms. Bundle your offers ("website + AI receptionist") and split the cost of finding clients.

## 5. Put it on paper

**From the text:** *"I know on paper but it doesn't matter in a practical sense."*

Notice that they *do* have paper. Families usually fall out in business over unclear expectations, not because family is bad. Shared bank accounts work for two brothers after decades together, but not on day one with a cousin.

- **Vesting.** If you give anyone equity, have it vest over time (a common structure is four years with a one-year cliff). That way a cousin who quits after three months doesn't own 10% forever.
- **Written splits.** Agree in writing on who gets what from each school's setup fee and monthly payment.
- **A one-page roles document.** Cover who decides pricing, who owns client relationships, and what happens if someone leaves.
- **A separate business bank account.** Pool deliberately instead of blurring everything together.

Have a lawyer or accountant set up anything involving equity.

## 6. Choose people who'd never stab you in the back, and test it first

**From the text:** *"As long as you're sure that they share your sentiments,"* plus the detail about Luke: he was brought in only *after* two years of struggle had shown what he was made of.

The Luke story is a test-before-trust story.

- **A paid trial project.** Have them build the agent's flow for "I want to cancel my membership." Watch the quality, and whether they deliver on time without being chased.
- **How they handle mistakes.** When the agent gives a wrong price during testing, do they tell you or quietly hide it?
- **Hunger over pedigree.** The friend delivering food who studies AI at night may beat the brilliant cousin who's coasting.
- **The martial arts world itself.** Adult assistant instructors who've been loyal to one school for years know which owners are drowning in missed calls. They make excellent referral partners.
- **Red flags.** Watch for someone who wants a title or equity before contributing anything, or who talks about other family members' money.

## Your first week

1. **Day 1:** List 20 people across the job categories in section 1.
2. **Day 2:** Have one conversation each with your top three. Swap the text's pitch for something like: *"I'm building AI receptionists for martial arts schools. I need someone to [specific task]. I'll pay [X] per [task], and if it works, there's more."*
3. **Days 3–5:** Give each person a paid trial task with a 24-hour deadline.
4. **Week 2:** Put roles and money splits in writing for whoever delivered.

To internalize this, make one line from the text your default question: *"How can we do this together?"* Every time a task comes up, ask who you already know who could do it or open a door, and what you could share instead of paying for twice.


The core idea in that text is **not actually "hire family because they're cheap."** The deeper principle is:

> **Leverage trusted relationships and underutilized talent before spending money on strangers.**

Some parts of the text are useful business strategy. Some parts are risky, legally questionable, or ethically weak (for example, relying on unpaid labor, manipulating people, or expecting family loyalty to replace professional systems).

For your business—**AI phone agents for martial arts schools**—the useful lesson is how to build a leverage network around yourself.
