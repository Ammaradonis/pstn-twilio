Below I've broken the text into its main ideas, tied each back to the original lines, and given martial arts school examples for each.

### 1. People don't buy unless you ask them to

> *"People don't buy things without being hard closed."*

School owners are some of the busiest small business owners around. They teach from 4 to 9pm, run belt tests, and handle billing and parent drama. When a sales call ends with "think it over," your proposal ends up buried. They didn't reject you. You just never asked them to decide. Every conversation needs to end with a direct question they can answer yes or no.

Soft endings versus hard closes:

- **After a live demo.** Soft: "I'll send you some info." Hard: "You just heard it book a trial class for a parent at 7pm on a Tuesday. Do you want that answering your phone, yes or no?"
- **After sending a proposal.** Soft: "Just checking in to see if you had questions!" Hard: "Are we moving ahead with the agent for your school, or should I close your file for now?"
- **At a seminar or expo.** Soft: "Here's my card, reach out anytime." Hard: "I can have this live on your number by next Friday. Want to book the setup call right now while we're both standing here?"

### 2. Urgency works, but only use the true kind

> *"You either have to do the false urgency thing 'we're running out of time' or... 'if you don't buy now, it's gonna be very hard to get this deal later on.'"*

The text is right that urgency drives decisions. But fake scarcity is especially risky for you, for three reasons:

- **The community is small.** Martial arts owners talk at seminars, in associations, in Facebook groups, and in franchise networks. If you tell three owners "last spot this month," it gets noticed.
- **You sell a subscription.** Someone pushed into buying by a fake deadline is more likely to cancel in month two.
- **It can be illegal.** Misleading scarcity claims can violate unfair commercial practice laws in many places.

The good news is that your business has plenty of real urgency:

- **The cost of waiting.** "You told me you miss about 15 calls a week while you're on the mat. If just one of those a week becomes a student paying 150 a month for a year, every week we wait costs you around 1,800 in lifetime revenue." This is your strongest argument, and it's always true.
- **Seasonal windows.** September back-to-school and January resolutions are peak inquiry periods for most schools. "It's mid-September. Parents are calling right now. Every week without the agent is this season's trial bookings going to voicemail." Or later in the year: "To be live and tested before the January rush, we need to start setup by early December."
- **Real capacity.** If you customize each agent yourself, cap how many schools you onboard per month. That makes "I have two onboarding slots left for October" true, and it improves your quality too.
- **A real price deadline.** "The founding-school rate ends on the 30th. After that it's the standard price." Then actually raise it on the 1st. If you don't, you've taught the market your deadlines mean nothing.

A simple test: if the prospect found out the full truth, would you be embarrassed about what you said? If yes, don't say it.

### 3. A definite beats a maybe

> *"It's better to... close five and lose five than sitting there on 10 that might drop in, might not drop in."*

The text names three costs of maybes: they "fuck with your estimated numbers," they create "maybe tomorrow we'll have money" thinking, and they waste your time.

- **Time.** Soft approach: 10 schools say "interested." You send follow-ups for six weeks, about 30 messages in total, and two eventually sign. Hard approach: you call all 10 this week and ask for a yes or no. Four say yes and six say no. You're done in one week, you have four clients, and your time is free for new leads.
- **Forecasting.** You can't plan how many agents to build in October, whether to hire help with setup, or how much to spend on ads when your pipeline is 10 maybes. With four signed schools, you can.
- **Cash flow.** "Maybe Coach Daniel signs this week" is not money. Four signed agreements with payment on file is money.

One practical change is to delete the "interested" stage from your pipeline. Every lead should be in one of three places: **Yes** (signed and paid), **No** (with a follow-up date), or **Decision meeting booked** (a specific date and time, with the actual decision-maker present).

### 4. Find out why they're a maybe

> *"WHY are they maybes? Make a phone call right now and find the fuck out."*

Every maybe has a hidden objection. Your job is to bring it into the open. The best question for this is: **"What would need to be true for this to be a yes today?"** Common answers from school owners, and how to respond:

- **"I'm worried parents will hate talking to a robot."** Have them call your demo line pretending to be a parent asking about kids' classes. Let the experience answer the objection instead of arguing with them.
- **"I need to talk to my wife" or "my business partner."** Many schools are family-run, so this is often real. Don't accept "I'll get back to you." Say: "Makes sense. Can we get 15 minutes with both of you Thursday at 9pm after classes?" This turns a maybe into a booked decision.
- **"After the tournament" or "after belt testing."** Ask: "What changes after testing?" Usually nothing does. If setup only needs a short amount of their time, tell them exactly how much, and offer to schedule it for the week after the event, with payment today.
- **"Money's tight right now."** Go back to the cost-of-waiting math. If the agent pays for itself with one extra enrollment a month, being low on money is a reason to start, not to wait.
- **"I tried an answering service before and it was bad."** Ask what specifically went wrong: wrong information, couldn't book trials, sounded scripted. Then show how your agent handles that exact failure.

### 5. The yes-or-no script

> *"We're filling up for the month... I want to do business with you... I understand you may not be ready... it's the last place I have available... I'm prepared to give you X percent discount. Yes or no?"*

The structure of that script is excellent. It gives context, says you want their business, acknowledges they may not be ready, gives a reason to act now, offers an incentive, and asks a direct question. Here it is adapted with real urgency:

**Capacity version** (only if you actually cap onboarding):
"Coach, I build every school's agent myself, so I only take on five schools a month, and I have one spot left for October. I think this would be great for your school. You're missing trial bookings every evening you're teaching. I get that you might not be ready, but if you are, I'll waive the setup fee to fill this spot. Yes or no?"

**Seasonal version:**
"Back-to-school is happening right now, and your phone is busiest while you're on the mat. I want to work with you. If we start today, you'll be live by next week and catch the rest of this enrollment wave. If not, we should plan for January. So do we start now, yes or no?"

**Written version** (for owners you can never reach by phone):
"Hi Coach Sarah, quick yes or no: do you want the agent live before your January enrollment push? If yes, I'll send the agreement today and we'll do setup the first week of December. If no, no problem, and I'll check back in the spring."

### 6. Take the no cleanly and move on

> *"Okay no problem. No problem. We'll be speaking in a few weeks. And then bang... move to the next, next, next."*

Notice the text doesn't say burn the bridge. It says "we'll be speaking in a few weeks." The point is to let go emotionally, not to be rude. A no isn't a failure. It frees up your time.

- **Give a dated follow-up.** "No problem at all. I'll call you on November 3rd, before you plan for January." Then put it in your calendar immediately.
- **Ask one question, then stop.** "Mind if I ask what made it a no?" Treat the answer as information for your next pitch. Don't argue with it.
- **Don't chase.** No "just circling back" message two days later. That turns a clean no back into a maybe, which is exactly what you're trying to avoid.
- **Expect some to return.** The owner who said no in September may call you in November when their front desk person quits. A respectful no leaves that door open.

### 7. Interest isn't a sale

> *"Maybe I'm a fucking astronaut. It doesn't mean anything until NASA calls me up and says 'get on the fuckin rocket.'"*

"Sounds great" is not a yes. For you, a definite should mean three things: **a signed agreement, a payment method on file, and a booked kickoff date.** Anything less is still a maybe.

- **"Send me the contract."** Don't just email it and hope. Say: "I'll send it now. Can you open it while we're on the phone?" Stay on the call while they sign.
- **A verbal yes at an event.** Book the onboarding call on your phone before you walk away from them.
- **"Let's do it next month."** Great. Take payment today and set the start date for next month. They've committed, and the timing works for them.

### 8. How to make this a habit

Knowing this isn't the hard part. Doing it every day is. The people you're selling to already understand this: they teach that repetition turns technique into reflex. Treat closing the same way.

- **Morning maybe sweep.** Every morning, list every open lead with no decision. Contact each one that day and get a yes, a no, or a booked decision meeting.
- **One rule for every call.** No call ends without a yes, a no, or a specific date and time for a decision. If you catch yourself saying "just let me know," you broke the rule.
- **Track your asks.** Count how many times per day you directly asked for the business, not just how many calls you made. Most people find they ask far less often than they think.
- **Rehearse out loud.** Say the scripts from section 5 ten times each, and record yourself. The first few times will feel pushy. By the tenth, "yes or no?" will just sound normal and friendly, which is how it lands best.
- **Weekly check.** Count how many leads have been maybes for longer than 14 days. Your goal is zero.
