I'll teach you the parts of this that actually made him money, and I'll tell you straight which parts didn't — because the text contradicts itself, and the contradiction is the whole lesson.

## The story he tells is not the story that happened

Read the Sky Sig story again carefully. What did he actually do?

He told a client he could get them on TV. He wasn't certain he could. He took the money. Then he **found a way and delivered.** The client ran an ad. It aired. It drove traffic to their site. They paid the second £150k *because it worked*.

That's not fraud. That's pre-selling with the execution risk sitting on his side of the table. The only "lie" was projecting certainty he didn't have — and he then made it true. He describes it as breaking rules; what he actually broke was his own fear of committing to something hard.

Now Fyre. McFarland took $26M and delivered nothing. Tate calls him a genius who "pushed it too far." But the line isn't *too far* — the line is **did anyone get what they paid for.** Sky Sig got an ad on air. Fyre ticketholders got a FEMA tent. One bought an Aston Martin, the other bought six years in federal prison and a $26M restitution order.

So I'm not going to teach you the "famouse the geese" part, and not on moral grounds alone — on the grounds that it's the part of his own text that demonstrably doesn't work, illustrated with his own example of a guy who's been to prison. The parts that work are below, and they're genuinely sharp.

## 1. Kill the reflex "no"

The real principle: when someone asks "can you do X," your default is *"Yes — here's what it costs and here's the timeline,"* not "no." You figure out the how afterward.

For your business:

- Dojo owner: *"Can it handle calls in German and Turkish?"* → "Yes. €X setup, live in two weeks." Then you go configure the multilingual routing. You didn't have it. Now you do.
- *"Can it push trial bookings straight into Kicksite/Zen Planner/Spark?"* → "Yes." Then you spend the weekend on their API or, if there's no API, you build a Zapier bridge, or worst case you email the owner a structured booking and let a VA key it in for the first month. Client doesn't care which. Outcome is identical to them.
- *"Can it stop giving out prices on the phone and just book the trial instead?"* → "Yes, that's actually the default behaviour I recommend." (It probably isn't yet. It will be by Friday.)
- *"Can it call back the 200 dead leads in my spreadsheet?"* → "Yes." Outbound is a different beast than inbound, but it's a beast you can build.

The honest version of "if you give me the money, I'll find a way" is: **yes, with a refund clause.** Take the money, commit to the outcome, and write "if it's not live and working in 30 days you get 100% back" into the contract. That sentence does two things at once: it closes deals faster than any certainty you could fake, *and* it removes the fraud problem entirely, because now you're risking your time instead of their money. Tate even says it himself — "worst case you give them their money back" — he just doesn't notice that this one line defuses the whole rest of his argument.

## 2. Sell it before you build it

This is the strongest thing in the text and it's fully legitimate.

- Don't build the Kicksite integration and *then* look for a school that wants it. Call thirty schools, find the four on Kicksite, sell the integration as a pilot at a discount for founding customers, *then* build it with their money in your account.
- Don't build a multi-tenant dashboard. Sell five schools, run all five off your own laptop and a spreadsheet, and only build the dashboard when the manual work starts hurting.
- Don't build the "handles kids' birthday party bookings" module. Mention it on three calls. If nobody bites, it doesn't exist.
- Charge a setup fee. €500–1500 upfront plus monthly. The upfront fee isn't just cash flow — it's the thing that proves they're serious and funds the build.

## 3. "He didn't have a festival, but he had a payment processor"

This is the best line in the whole piece and it's 100% correct. Order of operations for you:

- **Week one**: Stripe live, a phone number, one working demo agent, a calendar link. That's it.
- **Not week one**: logo, GmbH formation, a designed website, a brand name you love, business cards, a pitch deck, an accountant.
- Your website can be a single page with a "Call this number and talk to the agent" button. That demo *is* your sales material. A dojo owner who calls it and hears it book a trial class is 80% closed. No deck will beat that.
- Your first "case study" can be one school, three weeks, screenshot of their missed-call log before and after.

## 4. Constraint-driven creative (the actually clever bit of the Sky Sig ad)

Strip out the "I lied to the regulator" framing and what's left is a real skill: *when you can't say the obvious thing, make them curious enough to come find out.* That's craft, and it's legal.

For your outreach:

- Don't cold-email "Hi, I sell AI phone agents." Call the dojo during a class, let it ring out, then email: *"Just called at 18:40. Nobody picked up. That was me — how many of those were parents?"* That's curiosity plus a demonstrated pain point in two sentences.
- Send a 40-second recording of your agent handling a call *as their school*, with their name in it. Subject line: "I answered your phone for you." They will open it.
- At seminars and federation events: don't pitch. Ask owners how many trial enquiries they miss while they're on the mat. Let them do the math out loud. The number is always worse than they thought.

## 5. Cash in, speed, contempt for ceremony

Yes. Ignore the parts of business culture that are theatre. You do not need a mission statement, a Notion wiki, a brand book, an LLC in Delaware, or an advisory board. You need dojo owners paying you monthly. Most "how it's done" advice in business is people describing their own procrastination.

## Where this model actively breaks for *your* business specifically

Two reasons, both practical rather than moral.

**Your market is tiny and everyone knows each other.** Tate's model needs an infinite supply of anonymous strangers — that's literally what "the geese" means. You don't have that. There are maybe a few thousand serious martial arts schools in the DACH region, and their owners see each other at gradings, federation seminars, and tournaments constantly. It's one of the most referral-dense industries there is. Burn one BJJ academy in Vienna and you're finished in Austria inside a month — not because of ethics, because of *arithmetic*. The strategy requires a market you'll never see twice, and yours is a market you'll see every quarter for the next ten years.

**One rule in your business is genuinely load-bearing.** Not the email footer — he's right that nobody dies over a postal address. But you're building AI systems that talk to humans, in the EU, and as of 2 August this year Article 50 of the EU AI Act became enforceable, imposing direct transparency duties on providers and deployers of chatbots and voice AI agents — regardless of whether the system is high-risk. Providers must disclose that users are engaging with AI unless it's already obvious, and noncompliance can trigger fines of up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover.

On top of that: your agent records calls, handles parents' phone numbers, and handles data about *children* enrolled in kids' classes. That's GDPR special-category-adjacent territory. This isn't "the email police." This is the one place where a shortcut ends the company rather than costing you a slap.

And here's the thing — the disclosure is *free*. "Hi, this is the virtual assistant for Vienna BJJ" costs you nothing and converts identically. You're not giving anything up. Everything else in the compliance pile, sure, move fast and sort it later.

## Two scripts, so you can practise the yes

**When you can't do it yet:**
> "Yeah, we can do that. It's not something I have running out of the box today, so I'd build it for you — €X setup, live within three weeks. If it's not working by then you get the setup fee back, no argument. Want me to start?"

**When you genuinely can't do it:**
> "That one I can't do, but here's what I *can* do that solves the same problem —"

Learn the first one until it's reflex. That's the entire transferable core of everything in that text. The rest is a man telling you to admire someone in prison.

The passage is promoting a mindset that mixes a few useful entrepreneurial ideas with some genuinely bad ones.

If you try to internalize it literally, you'll eventually damage your reputation, lose clients, get sued, or destroy your business. The valuable part is not "break the rules." The valuable part is understanding what successful entrepreneurs often see that rule-followers miss.

For an AI phone agent business serving martial arts schools, here's how I would translate the useful principles into something that actually works.

# Principle 1: Don't confuse regulations with limitations

What Tate is really describing:

> Most people assume that because something is difficult, unusual, or uncommon, it can't be done.

That's different from fraud.

### Bad interpretation

A school asks:

> Can your AI book trials, follow up leads, answer questions, recover lost leads, and increase memberships by 50%?

You say:

> Absolutely guaranteed.

When you have no idea if it can.

### Better interpretation

You say:

> Yes, we can build a system designed to do those things. Let me show you how.

You sell the outcome, then solve the problem.

### Examples

#### Example 1

School owner:

> Can an AI answer calls at midnight?

Most competitors:

> That's not how martial arts schools operate.

You:

> Yes. That's exactly when leads are often lost.

#### Example 2

School owner:

> Can AI call every missed lead from the last 3 years?

Others:

> That's too much work.

You:

> Upload the spreadsheet.

#### Example 3

School owner:

> Can AI respond in English and Spanish?

Others:

> We'd need another staff member.

You:

> The AI can switch languages automatically.

You're not breaking laws.

You're breaking assumptions.

# Principle 2: Sell the result before perfecting the system

One thing many entrepreneurs get wrong:

They spend months building.

They spend days selling.

The Fyre Festival founder did this in an unethical way.

But there is a useful lesson.

### Wrong

Spend 6 months building the perfect AI agent.

No customers.

No revenue.

### Better

Sell first.

Build second.

### Examples

#### Example 1

Call 100 martial arts schools.

Offer:

> We install an AI receptionist that answers every missed call within 60 seconds.

If 20 schools say yes, now build and refine.

#### Example 2

Create a landing page.

Offer:

> AI Enrollment Assistant for Martial Arts Schools.

Run ads.

Measure demand.

Build based on actual customer needs.

#### Example 3

Get a signed agreement first.

Then customize the workflow.

Many software companies operate this way.

# Principle 6: Separate "rules that matter" from "rules that don't"

This is where many people misunderstand the message.

Some rules are bureaucracy.

Some rules protect people.

Treating them the same is dangerous.

### Rules that matter

* contracts
* privacy laws
* consumer protection laws
* billing honesty
* data security

Never break these.

### Rules that often matter less early on

* perfect branding
* perfect website
* perfect internal processes
* perfect CRM setup

These can wait.

# Principle 7: Sell confidence, not certainty

A hidden theme in the story:

The customer bought confidence.

People buy certainty.

### Instead of

> I think our AI might help.

Say:

> We've built systems specifically to stop schools from losing leads. Let me show you exactly how.

### Instead of

> Maybe we can integrate with your CRM.

Say:

> We'll figure out the integration path together. We've solved similar cases before.

One sounds hesitant.

One sounds capable.
