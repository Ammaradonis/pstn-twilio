## First, notice what you're actually selling

The lesson lands harder in your business than almost any other, because your product *is* the thesis. A martial arts school owner used to need a front-desk person answering calls from 9 to 9. Now one AI agent handles the trial bookings, the "how much is it" calls, the parents asking if the 6pm kids class has spots. "One man with a laptop and a phone can get a lot of shit done" is literally your sales pitch.

So if you build a bloated company to sell a leverage product, you're a hypocrite and your clients will feel it. Three applications:

- **Run your own agent on your own line.** Your inbound calls, your demo bookings, your renewal reminders. It saves you a hire and it becomes the demo. "Call this number, that's my agent doing my sales, it's the same thing you're buying."
- **Every manual task is a product gap.** If someone on your team is spending four hours a week manually configuring class schedules for new schools, that's not a job to hire for, that's a setup wizard to build. The army version hires an onboarding team. The special forces version ships a template library and one person runs it.
- **Sell the same math to gym owners.** A school owner with three part-time front desk staff is running an army. Your pitch to them is the exact lesson you're learning.

## Attention is the constraint, not money

The 72 girls didn't fail because he ran out of cash. He was doing 600k a month. They failed because half of them didn't know who he was. Human attention doesn't scale, and he'd built a machine that required more of it than existed.

You'll hit this in two places, and most people only see the first one.

**Staff.** You can properly coach maybe three to five people. Weekly one-on-ones, listening to their calls with them, actually knowing what they're stuck on. At eight you're managing managers and you've become an HR department for a company that doesn't need one.

**Clients.** This is the version that will actually kill you. Martial arts schools are high-touch. The owner is a black belt, not a technologist, and he wants to text you when the agent mispronounces "kumite." Consider:

- 150 schools at $297/month with four support staff, 12% monthly churn, and you hate your life.
- 45 schools at $997/month where you personally tune every agent, near-zero churn, and one support person.

Second one makes more money, takes a quarter of the overhead, and you actually enjoy it. That's the "fired everyone, kept the top ten" moment translated into your business. His top ten girls outproduced the 72 because they finally got his attention.

Practical test: if you can't name every client's head instructor and their busiest class time, you have more clients than attention.

## Turnover is vanity. Profit per head is the score

He turned over 600 grand and it was "crazy," and it was also worse than the smaller version. Huge percentages, a management structure, rents everywhere. Revenue was a number he could say out loud. Profit was the number that mattered.

Pick one scoreboard and put it where you can see it: **profit divided by number of people, including you.**

- Four people, $40k/month profit. That's $10k per head. Fine.
- Two people, $35k/month profit. That's $17.5k per head. Much better business, smaller number to brag about.
- One person, $28k/month. $28k per head. This is probably where you should be for a while.

Every hire has to clear that bar or it drags the business backwards. Ask it directly: does this person add more profit than they dilute?

## Hire stackers, not specialists

"Multifaceted individuals, people who can do more than one thing." In a company of five this isn't a nice-to-have, it's structural. A specialist in a five-person company is idle 60% of the time.

Concrete stacks for your business:

- **Sales + onboarding.** The person who sold the school does the setup call. They know exactly what was promised, so the agent matches the pitch. No handoff, no "well the sales guy said it could do X."
- **Prompt/voice engineering + escalated support.** Whoever tunes the agents also hears the complaints firsthand. They stop shipping the failure mode instead of ticketing it.
- **Support + QA + documentation.** Someone reviewing call transcripts for a client is already doing QA. Have them write the help doc from what they find.
- **The unfair hire: someone who actually trains.** A BJJ brown belt or a muay thai coach who can sell. They walk into a gym and they're a peer, not a vendor. They understand why the owner can't answer the phone at 6:45pm. That one person replaces a salesperson plus a customer success person plus a marketing consultant who "does the martial arts vertical."

Add one filter when hiring: **does this person turn their work into reusable assets, or just complete tasks?** The special forces trait in a software business is that today's work makes next month's work smaller. Someone who onboards 10 schools and hands you a template library is worth five people who onboard 10 schools each and hand you nothing.

## Be ruthless correctly

"Fire anyone who's shit" is right in direction and dangerous in practice, because most people use it as a way to avoid looking at their own systems. He half-admits this himself: he says the staff weren't "motivated properly," and he was the one doing the motivating. The 72 weren't lazy. They were unmanaged.

So run it in this order:

1. **Ruthless at the gate.** Paid trial project, two to four weeks, real work. Have a candidate configure an agent for a real school and get it live. You'll learn more in two weeks than in five interviews. Most bad fires are bad hires.
2. **Define the one number they own.** Setter owns qualified demos booked per week. Onboarder owns days-from-signature-to-live-agent. Support owns first-contact resolution and monthly churn. If a role can't own a number, it probably shouldn't be a role.
3. **Fire fast on reliability and honesty. Coach on skill.** Someone who hides a broken deployment is gone. Someone whose prompts are mediocre in month one can be taught.
4. **The re-hire test, monthly.** Knowing what you know now, would you hire this person again today? If you hesitate, you already have your answer and you're just paying interest on it.
5. **Ruthless with yourself first.** If two people in a row failed the same role, the role is broken, the onboarding is broken, or you're an unclear manager. Check that before you blame the third.

## Manage the small team completely differently

He's right that "they cannot be managed the same," and this is where people build an elite team and then destroy it. Armies run on procedure because the people are interchangeable. Special forces run on intent because they aren't.

What that means for you:

- **Brief outcomes and constraints, not task lists.** "Get time-to-live under five days without adding cost, you own how" instead of a 14-step checklist. If you hire someone great and then micromanage them, they leave and you're back to an army.
- **Debriefs, not standups.** Weekly, honest, what broke and why. Listen to three real client calls together every week.
- **Open the numbers.** With three people you can show MRR, churn, and profit. People who see the scoreboard behave like owners. He tried to motivate 72 people with money alone and it was "very very expensive." Money is the only lever that works at scale. Ownership, access, and mission work at small scale and cost less.
- **Pay the top of the market for fewer people.** One person at $8k who does the work of three beats three at $3.5k. Same spend, less management, better output.

## Where this advice will bite you

Take the lesson, but hold these three in the other hand.

- **Lean has a floor.** If you're doing support tickets at 11pm and haven't shipped a feature in six weeks, you're not lean, you're the bottleneck. The goal is high output per head, not the lowest possible headcount.
- **The top-ten strategy concentrates risk.** If 40 schools are your whole business, losing four is losing 10% of revenue. He carried that risk too. Keep a real pipeline even when you're full, and don't let any client exceed roughly 10% of revenue.
- **Key-person risk on the technical side.** If one person holds the entire prompt architecture and telephony setup in their head, you don't have a special forces unit, you have a hostage situation. Documentation isn't bureaucracy at this size, it's insurance.

## What to do this week

1. **List every function in the business.** Lead gen, demos, agent config, number porting, QA, support, billing, content. Next to each, mark: only I can do it, needs a great human, or should be software/outsourced. Most of what you'd hire for lands in the third bucket.
2. **Calculate profit per head today.** Write it on the wall. Any hire has to raise it.
3. **Rank your clients by profit, then by pain.** Find the bottom 20%. Either raise their price to where they're worth the attention, or let them go. This is the "fired nearly all of them, kept the top ten" move, applied where it'll pay you fastest.
4. **Run the re-hire test on anyone currently working with you**, including contractors.
5. **Before your next hire, ask if an existing person plus a tool can absorb it.** If yes, that's the answer.

The line to keep is the one he ends on: wanting a big team is vanity. In your business specifically, it's also a contradiction. You sell the idea that one great operator with the right software beats a room full of people. Be the proof.

The core lesson is not literally “small teams are always better.” The deeper principle is:

**Optimize for output per person, not number of people.**

In your AI phone agent business for martial arts schools, the biggest mistake would be measuring growth by:

* Number of employees
* Number of contractors
* Number of clients
* Number of agents deployed

Instead, measure:

* Revenue per employee
* Revenue per client
* Profit per client
* Customer success per support hour
* Number of schools managed per team member

That's the "special forces" mindset.

Most founders unconsciously build a company where every new customer requires hiring another person.

Special forces businesses do the opposite.

## Army Model

10 schools = 1 account manager

20 schools = 2 account managers

40 schools = 4 account managers

80 schools = 8 account managers

Growth creates complexity.

---

## Special Forces Model

10 schools = 1 account manager

40 schools = 1 account manager

100 schools = 1 account manager

200 schools = 2 account managers

Growth creates leverage.

> "How many missed calls did we have yesterday?"

* Staff member checks dashboard

* Client logs in
* Gets answer themselves

One system replaces hundreds of support interactions.

* Email support
* Staff changes settings

Special forces approach:

* Self-service portal
* Owner changes settings

Again, software replaces labor.
