## The translation

The line to build everything on is "if you have to go somewhere, try and go somewhere for business." For you that's unusually easy, because your customer is a physical building full of people who are obsessed with the same thing you can plausibly be obsessed with. Martial arts school owners are almost impossible to reach by phone (that's literally why your product exists) and almost trivially easy to reach in person, on a mat, at 7pm, sweaty, with no gatekeeper.

So the "mix everything together" instruction for you isn't abstract. It means: your training, your travel, your weekends, and your social circle all sit on top of your pipeline if you point them at it.

---

## "Make sure the people around you, your friends are talking about business"

- Audit the five people you speak to most in a week. Count how many run something. If it's zero, that's the first fix, before any tactic below.
- Build two rooms, not one. Room A is full of your customers: MATA, the Facebook groups where school owners complain about billing and retention, franchise owner groups (Gracie Barra, ATA, Premier, 10th Planet affiliates all have their own back channels). Room B is full of your peers: other people building voice agents or selling software into local service businesses. Room A gives you objections and language. Room B gives you the technical and sales playbooks.
- Don't join Room A to sell. Join it and answer phone/front-desk questions for free for three months until you're "the phone guy." In an industry this word-of-mouth, reputation moves faster than outbound.
- Make one of your recurring social slots a business slot. A weekly call with three other founders selling to local businesses (HVAC, dental, med spa) will teach you more about your own motion than any martial arts specific content, because the missed-call problem is identical across all of them.

## "Try and go for dinner with some guys you need to talk to about business"

- Upgrade from attending dinners to hosting them. The night before a local tournament, book a table for eight and invite school owners. You're not pitching, you're convening. The person who convenes the room owns the room.
- Have dinner one layer out from your customer: gym software resellers (Kicksite, Zen Planner, PushPress, Spark), mat and gi suppliers, the local marketing agencies already running ads for schools. Each of those people has 100 to 500 schools in their book. One good dinner is a channel partnership, not one customer.
- Post-belt-testing and post-seminar dinners are the highest-density version of this. Owners are there, they're happy, and nobody is guarding their calendar.
- When you have dinner you didn't choose (family, partner, friends), choose the restaurant. Put it near a school you want to walk into at 5:30pm on the way.

## "Could you go to India... and speak to another bouncy ball company?"

The point of the Seychelles/India swap isn't the destination, it's asking "what does this location give the business that another one wouldn't?" Your three possible answers are prospect density, cheap capable talent, and a testbed.

- Prospect density: pick trip cities by what's happening there. Big tournaments (IBJJF opens, ADCC trials, NAGA, national TKD/karate events) and industry events like the Martial Arts SuperShow pull hundreds of owners into one place. Check the calendar before you pick dates, not after. Verify the current year's dates directly, they move.
- Testbed: Thailand is the obvious one for you. Muay Thai camps run on inbound tourist enquiries by phone and WhatsApp, in a second language, at all hours, and they miss an enormous share of them. Same trip, and you come home with three international case studies and a multilingual version of your product.
- Talent: if you want a beach, pick one attached to a labour pool you'd actually hire from. A week that produces one good setter or one voice engineer has paid for itself.
- Everyday version: choose your ski trip, surf trip, or family visit town, then search "martial arts school [town]" and book three coffees before you fly. You were going anyway. The flight cost is already sunk.

## "I told all my cam girls, if we make this much money this month, I'll take you all to Thailand"

The mechanic underneath that story: tie a reward your team wants to a number the business needs, and pick a reward you were buying anyway.

- With a team: "We get 25 schools live by June and we all go to [city] for the tournament weekend." They get a trip. You get a market blitz in your densest prospect cluster.
- Solo: run it on yourself. The trip doesn't get booked until ten schools are paying. You'll be amazed how fast month one moves.
- One adjustment I'd make. Run it transparently. Tell the team it's a work trip with a great weekend attached. In martial arts, where everyone knows everyone within two degrees, being the operator who quietly engineers people is a reputation you cannot outrun. The incentive works just as well when it's honest, and it survives the team finding out.

## "Hustlers never turn their brain off of the money"

This is the part that actually compounds, and it costs you nothing.

- Every phone call you make to any business is R&D. Call a dentist, a salon, a pizza place. Notice who answers, how long it rings, what they say first. Keep a running note of good and bad openings. Your best agent scripts will come from there.
- Call twenty martial arts schools at 6:30pm on a Tuesday and record what happens. Voicemail, a kid at the desk, dead air. That recording is your single best sales asset. Play an owner his own missed call and you don't need a pitch.
- Your own training session is fieldwork. Watch what happens when a walk-in comes through the door mid-class. Watch the front desk fumble. That's your demo, observed live.
- Fill the dead time. Driving, cooking, walking: gym-owner podcasts and martial arts business content instead of anything else. You're building vocabulary, and sounding like an insider is most of the sale in this niche.

## Where I'd adapt it

"If I'm awake, I'm working" is a good description of attention and a bad description of labour. The version that works is: your noticing never turns off, your output does.

There's also a specific reason to adapt it in your case. Your entire pitch to a school owner is "you can't answer at 6pm because you're teaching, so stop trying to be reachable 24/7 and let the agent handle it." Running your own life on the opposite principle is off-message, and more practically, you sell to athletes. Turning up to an open mat gassed and grey is the worst sales call you'll ever make in this industry.

Keep the attention permanently on. Keep the calendar intentionally mixed. Sleep anyway.

The core idea in that passage is not "work 24/7 until you burn out."

The useful principle is:

> **Reduce the separation between life and opportunity.**
>
> Train yourself to naturally notice business opportunities, conversations, relationships, information, and leverage in situations that other people treat as unrelated to work.

For your business—AI phone agents for martial arts schools—that mindset can be extremely powerful if applied intelligently.

The goal is not to become obsessed with work every second. The goal is to develop a habit where many normal activities also produce business value.

# 1. Turn Every Conversation Into Market Research

The quote says:

> "If you go for dinner, go for dinner with people you need to talk to about business."

Applied to your business:

Instead of treating conversations as completely separate from business, learn to collect information constantly.

### Example 1: Talking to a martial arts instructor

Normal person:

> "Cool gym. Nice."

You:

> "What's the biggest headache when students call?"
>
> "How many calls go unanswered?"
>
> "When do most missed calls happen?"
>
> "Do leads usually call or fill out forms?"

You may discover:

* They miss calls after 5 PM
* Parents call during classes
* Trial bookings are inconsistent
* Staff hate answering phones

That's sales intelligence.

### Example 2: Talking to gym owners socially

At an event:

Instead of discussing random topics for an hour:

* Ask how they get leads
* Ask retention rates
* Ask what software they use
* Ask who answers phones

You might learn that 10 gyms all have the same problem.

Now your offer becomes stronger.

### Example 3: Existing clients

Every client call becomes:

* customer support
* product research
* testimonial collection
* referral generation

One conversation serves four objectives.

That's exactly what "mix everything together" means.

# The Highest-Leverage Interpretation

The deepest lesson isn't:

> "Work every waking second."

The deepest lesson is:

> **Design your life so that one activity accomplishes multiple objectives simultaneously.**

For your AI phone-agent company:

A single lunch could be:

* relationship building
* market research
* sales
* partnership development
* content creation

A single trip could be:

* recreation
* networking
* lead generation
* client retention
* industry research

A single client call could be:

* support
* upsell discovery
* testimonial gathering
* referral generation

When you start stacking outcomes like this, your business grows much faster because you're no longer doing one thing at a time—you are extracting value from the same hour in multiple ways.
