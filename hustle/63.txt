## What's genuinely true, and how to run it

**"Money In is primary" really means: shorten your cash conversion cycle.**

Your business has an unusually good structure for this. Martial arts schools bill members monthly and have predictable revenue. They can prepay. Your costs are usage-based and billed in arrears. That gap is where you live.

Concrete moves:

- **Annual prepay at 2 months free.** School pays €2,500 today instead of €250/month. You get 10 months of runway per customer instantly. Do this on every renewal and every new deal above your entry tier.
- **Non-refundable onboarding fee, €500 to €1,500.** Charge for call-flow configuration, scripting their trial-class offer, number porting, CRM integration. This covers your CAC before you spend a euro on delivery.
- **Get vendor terms in arrears while you collect in advance.** Twilio, Vapi/Retell, ElevenLabs, your LLM provider all bill you monthly after usage. If your customers prepay annually and your vendors invoice you 30 days later, your working capital requirement is negative. That's the real version of "debt isn't scary."
- **Kill net-30 for schools.** SEPA direct debit (Lastschrift) or card on file, charged on day one of the period. A martial arts school owner is not going to fight you on this because it's how they bill their own students.

That's the entire lesson, implemented legally, and it produces more cash than stiffing anyone would.

## Debt as growth capital: the actual test

"Get in debt if it's gonna bring money in" is right, but it needs a hurdle, or it's just a vibe.

The test: **does €1 of borrowed money return more than €1 plus the cost of capital, and how fast?**

Run your numbers. Something like:

- School pays €250/month. Gross margin after telephony, STT/TTS, and LLM costs is maybe 70%, so €175/month contribution.
- SMB churn of 4%/month means average life around 25 months. LTV ≈ €4,375 contribution.
- If your fully loaded CAC is €900, payback is roughly 5 months and LTV:CAC is nearly 5:1.

At those numbers you should borrow aggressively to fund acquisition, because every €900 you deploy returns €4,375 and you get your money back inside half a year. That's the defensible version of the mindset. What you're borrowing against is a measured, repeatable conversion process, not hope.

Where to get it, in Austria, without a bank:

- **aws (Austria Wirtschaftsservice)** has guarantee and startup financing programmes. **FFG** funds R&D, and building German-language conversational agents for a vertical plausibly qualifies.
- **Revenue-based financing** against MRR. European providers like re:cap, Karmen, and Uncapped will advance against recurring contracts. Expensive on paper, but non-dilutive and fast.
- **Customer-funded debt**, which is the best kind. The annual prepay above is an interest-free loan from your customers.

What not to borrow for: payroll with no pipeline, a rebrand, an office, or "more AI credits" without a signed contract behind them.

## Debt negotiation: the legitimate version works fine

The landlord story is the one tactic in the text that isn't fantasy. Creditors do settle, because recovery rates minus collection costs make 40 cents today rational versus 100 cents in 18 months.

But do it truthfully, because the lie is what creates the criminal exposure, not the discount.

- **Vendor overage disputes.** If you get hit with an unexpected €8,000 LLM or telephony bill from a runaway loop, call before the due date. "We had a technical fault, here's the log, we can pay €4,000 now and we're a growing account." You'll often get a credit. You get zero of that if you ignored four invoices first.
- **Genuine cash crunch.** Austria has a real out-of-court settlement process (außergerichtlicher Ausgleich), and in formal restructuring proceedings there are statutory minimum quotas, roughly 20% or 30% depending on the procedure. So creditors know what they'd get in a formal process, and they'll often take slightly more than that voluntarily. That's your negotiating anchor, and it's a factual one.
- **Timing, not stiffing.** "We can pay in full on the 15th instead of the 1st" is a request nearly everyone grants. "We're not paying until you sue" is a request nobody forgets.

## Why vendor-stiffing specifically destroys *your* business

This is the part I'd tattoo somewhere. The flashlight guy could stiff a supplier because the next supplier was a booth 40 metres away at the same trade show. Your suppliers are utilities with switching costs measured in weeks, and switching them means an outage across your entire customer base simultaneously.

- **Twilio or your CPaaS suspends you** for non-payment. Every client's phone line goes dark at once. Porting numbers out takes weeks per number, and you'd be doing it for every school you serve, during an outage, while they're all calling you.
- **Carrier registration.** Your A2P/campaign registrations and caller-ID reputation are tied to your account. Lose the account, lose the registration, start the approval queue from zero.
- **LLM provider revokes your key.** The agent stops mid-call. Not degraded. Stopped. While a prospective student is trying to book a free trial class.
- **AWS or your hosting suspends.** You're on a countdown to data deletion, holding call recordings for clients under GDPR obligations you can't meet.

A supplier you can't replace inside 48 hours without customer-visible failure is not a supplier you can play games with. Pay them early if anything.

Martial arts school ownership is a village. Gracie Barra, ATA, Tiger Schulmann franchisees, the Zen Planner and Kicksite user groups, the MAIA SuperShow crowd, German and Austrian Verband circles. They all talk, and school owners are famously referral-driven because they distrust software vendors already.

A bad Bonität rating means vendors demand Vorauskasse. Prepayment destroys exactly the float advantage described in the first section. You'd be sabotaging the one real lesson in the document.

## On "limited liability means the debts aren't yours"

In Austria this is much weaker than the text claims, and you should know exactly where it breaks:

- **Personal guarantee.** Any Austrian bank lending to a young GmbH will ask for a Bürgschaft. Sign it and the limited liability is gone for that debt. This makes the entire $3M passage a fantasy in the first place.
- **Taxes and social security.** A Geschäftsführer is personally liable for unremitted payroll tax and ÖGK contributions. The veil doesn't just pierce, it isn't there.
- **Insolvenzverschleppung.** Once the company is insolvent or over-indebted, you have a statutory deadline (in Austria, 60 days) to file. Trading past it creates personal liability to creditors who were harmed.
- **Hiding assets from creditors** is a criminal offence under the Austrian penal code, with sentences that scale with the damage amount. That's the $2.5M-in-a-personal-account plan, named.
- **Phoenixing** with the same website and phone number is the most detectable version of it. An Insolvenzverwalter looks specifically for continuity of business. And your customers would see it too.

Please verify the specifics with a Steuerberater and a Rechtsanwalt. I'm not a lawyer. But the shape is right: in Austria the liability shield is real for ordinary commercial risk and useless for deliberate creditor harm.

## The Core Lesson Hidden Inside the Rant

The actual lesson is not:

> "Debt isn't real."

The actual lesson is:

> "Cash flow determines whether a business survives."

A business can survive with debt.

A business cannot survive without cash coming in.

That distinction is crucial.
