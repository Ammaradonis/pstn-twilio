## The part the lesson leaves out

The lesson is right that the second rep is cheaper than the first. But it isn't automatic. Plenty of people onboard their 40th client exactly as slowly as their 4th, because the learning stayed in their head instead of becoming a thing they can reuse.

The exponent comes from one habit: **every first rep must produce an artifact.** A template, a checklist, a script, a form, a recording. The rep is the tuition. The artifact is the degree. If you finish a hard first rep with nothing written down, you paid the tuition and skipped the class.

So the practical translation of lesson 58 for you is: do the hard thing once, slowly, and leave behind something that makes the next one fast.

## Step 1: Write down your repeatable units

You can't compound what you haven't named. In your business the repeated units are roughly:

- Booking a demo with a school owner
- Running the demo
- Closing and collecting payment
- Intake (schedule, pricing, trial offer, FAQs, staff names)
- Building the agent's script and knowledge base
- CRM integration (Kicksite, Zen Planner, Gymdesk, PushPress, Spark, Mindbody)
- Number setup, call forwarding, compliance registration
- Testing before go-live
- Go-live and week-one monitoring
- Monthly ROI reporting to the owner
- Handling "your agent said the wrong thing" escalations
- Churn saves
- Hiring and training a setup tech

Every one of those is a staircase you will climb hundreds of times. Each is somewhere between rep 1 and rep 50 right now.

## Step 2: The second-time rule

Never do anything twice from scratch. The second time you hit a task, you build the reusable version before you do the work.

Concrete examples from your business:

**Intake.** Rep 1: three phone calls with the owner, you forget to ask about summer camp, the agent books a 6-year-old into the adult no-gi class, owner is furious, go-live slips eleven days. Artifact: a single intake form that captures every field you missed. Rep 5: one call plus the form. Rep 20: owner fills it themselves, a tech builds it, you never touch it.

**The agent script.** Rep 1: written from nothing over a week. Artifact: a martial-arts master template with the twelve questions every dojo gets — trial class booking, what age can my kid start, do you have adult classes, pricing, contracts and freezes, belt testing fees, make-up classes, holiday closures, is it good for bullying, do you do women's self-defence. Rep 6: fill in the blanks, ninety minutes.

**CRM integration.** Rep 1 on Zen Planner: four hours of guessing at their API and a support ticket. Artifact: a one-page Zen Planner playbook with the exact steps and the two things that break. Rep 4 on Zen Planner: twenty minutes, done by someone who isn't you.

**Testing.** Rep 1: you go live and the owner finds the bugs. Artifact: a fixed 30-call test script you run before every launch. Rep 10: a VA runs it and sends you a pass/fail sheet.

**Objections.** The first time an owner says "my front desk already answers the phone," you fumble. Artifact: the counter, backed by your own client call logs showing what share of calls land while the coach is on the mat or after 8pm. By rep 30 you're not discovering the objection, you're routing it.

**Hiring.** First hire: six weeks, bad fit. Artifact: job ad, plus a paid test task ("build an agent for this fictional dojo from this intake form, three hours"), plus training videos you recorded while doing the work anyway. Third hire: eight days.

## Step 3: Your niche is the exponent

This is the most underrated thing in your setup. He says "you understand mechanisms." Mechanisms only transfer when the next job looks like the last one.

If you sold AI phone agents to dentists, HVAC companies, salons and law firms, every rep would reset. Different caller intent, different software, different objections, different regulations. You'd be doing rep 1 forever.

Martial arts schools all share the same shape: the primary conversion goal is a booked trial class, the caller is usually a parent asking about a child, the schedule is organised by age and belt, the seasonal spikes are September and January, and everyone asks about contracts. School #30 is nearly identical to school #12. That's why your curve can be steep.

So the correct reading of "I launch a company a day" is not *start more businesses*. For you it's *launch a client a day*. Branching into a second vertical right now would reset your exponent to zero and cost you the one advantage you have.

## Step 4: Count reps, not months

This is where lesson 1 plugs in. The curve is driven by rep count, not calendar time. Two founders can both spend a year on this and one has done 15 onboardings while the other has done 120.

- If onboarding takes two weeks and you run them one at a time, you get 26 reps a year.
- If you cut it to three days and run four in parallel, you get well over 100.

Same year, four times the learning. So every hour you spend shortening the cycle buys compounding twice: the job gets cheaper *and* you climb the staircase faster.

Practical version: track cycle time per unit. Demo-to-close days. Close-to-live days. Hours per build. If a number isn't dropping across reps, you're repeating without capturing.

## Step 5: Know what the next "hard" looks like

He's honest that the problems change rather than disappear. Naming yours in advance stops them feeling like failure when they arrive.

- **At 5 schools:** cash. Owners haggling. You doing everything yourself.
- **At 30:** support load. One bad call becomes a refund demand. You're the bottleneck on every escalation.
- **At 100:** telephony and model costs eating margin, carrier compliance paperwork, churn you can't see coming, a model update quietly changing how your agents behave, competitors selling the same thing at half price, and staff you have to actually manage.

None of those mean the curve broke. They mean you climbed. The version of you at 100 schools has problems, but as he says, being broke isn't one of them.

## Step 6: Delegation is the final form

"James do this, Dylan do this" is not charisma. It's what happens when every step of the launch exists as a written checklist someone else can execute. Your goal state is a launch runbook so complete that a competent person with no martial arts knowledge can take a school live without asking you a question.

Until it's written down, it isn't compounding, it's just your memory. And memory doesn't scale, doesn't delegate, and walks out the door if you get sick.

## Step 7: Decide your quit point now

The 99% quit during the hard part because they judge by feeling and feelings arrive early. Replace the feeling with a number. "I will run 100 demos before I judge this offer." "I will onboard 25 schools before I decide the model doesn't work." Precommit while you're calm, so the decision isn't made on a bad Tuesday in month four.
